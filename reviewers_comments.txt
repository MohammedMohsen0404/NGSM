Reviewer 1

The manuscript entitled “An Edge-Centric Smart Metering System for Privacy-Preserving Energy Analytics and Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring” presents an edge-centric smart metering system, termed NGSM, that integrates embedded electrical metrology, Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring, harmonic analysis, cloud-based forecasting, and web/mobile user interfaces. The principal technical component is an Enhanced Dilated Temporal Convolutional Network deployed on an Allwinner F1C200s platform containing a single ARM926EJ-S core, 64 MB RAM, and no GPU or floating-point unit. The proposed EDTCN uses six exponentially dilated residual blocks, a 127-timestep receptive field, and a device-type-weighted focal loss. The authors additionally apply compiler-level optimization and INT8 quantization for embedded execution.
The manuscript addresses an important and practically relevant problem. Deploying NILM directly on low-cost smart meters could reduce the transmission of high-resolution household consumption data and decrease dependence on expensive GPU-enabled platforms. The hardware development across three generations, the implementation on a highly resource-constrained processor, and the use of multiple public NILM datasets are positive aspects. The paper also reports detailed hardware measurements, including memory utilization, power consumption, and inference performance.
Nevertheless, the present manuscript requires major revision. The paper attempts to cover an unusually broad range of components; metering hardware, embedded Linux, NILM, harmonic analysis, forecasting, cloud services, payment systems, mobile applications, security, privacy, and federated adaptation. As a result, several central scientific claims are not validated with sufficient rigor. In particular, the cross-dataset generalization protocol is unclear, the proposed loss and EDTCN contributions are not isolated adequately, embedded inference latency appears incompatible with some real-time claims, privacy is asserted mainly through local processing rather than formally evaluated, and the metrology and IEEE 519 compliance claims lack complete laboratory validation. Some reported numerical comparisons and cost statements are also internally inconsistent.
The work has potential as a substantial engineering system paper, but stronger methodological isolation, reproducible validation, and more cautious interpretation are necessary.
1) The NILM evaluation protocol and the meaning of “cross-dataset generalization” must be clarified rigorously. The manuscript reports results on SustDataED2, REFIT, and UK-DALE and repeatedly describes these findings as evidence of cross-dataset generalization. However, it is not sufficiently clear whether: one model was trained on one dataset and tested directly on another; separate models were independently trained and tested on each dataset; models were pretrained on one dataset and fine-tuned on another; or the three datasets were pooled during training.
Evaluating separately trained models on three datasets is multi-dataset validation, not necessarily cross-dataset generalization. True cross-domain evaluation should train on one or more source datasets and test on an unseen target dataset without target-household training.
The authors should provide a complete experimental matrix showing: training dataset; validation dataset; testing dataset; selected houses; appliance mappings; sampling rates; resampling procedures; time periods; and whether target-domain data were used for adaptation.
The following evaluations should be added: train on REFIT and test directly on UK-DALE; train on UK-DALE and test on REFIT; leave-one-house-out evaluation; unseen-house evaluation within each dataset; zero-shot testing before adaptation; performance after limited target-house adaptation.
Without these experiments, the claim of robust cross-dataset generalization should be revised to multi-dataset performance evaluation.
2) The methodological novelty of EDTCN and the weighted focal loss is not isolated adequately. Dilated temporal convolution, residual blocks, focal loss, class weighting, and quantization are established techniques. The manuscript combines these elements, but it does not clearly establish which modification is responsible for the reported improvement.
The authors should provide a controlled ablation study including: standard TCN; TCN with residual connections; TCN with exponential dilation; TCN with the proposed receptive field; TCN with conventional mean-squared or mean-absolute loss; TCN with standard focal loss; TCN with class-weighted loss; TCN with the proposed device-type-weighted focal loss; FP32 EDTCN; and INT8 EDTCN.
The mathematical formulation of the device-type-weighted focal loss should be presented explicitly. The manuscript should define: appliance-specific weights; positive and negative class weights; focusing parameter; regression and classification components; threshold selection; normalization; and how the weights are derived.
It is also unclear how focal loss, conventionally formulated for classification, is combined with appliance power regression. The manuscript should clarify whether separate output heads are used for state classification and power estimation.
A component-level statistical comparison is needed to demonstrate that EDTCN is more than a standard dilated TCN with empirical weighting.
3) The “real-time” embedded execution claim is not sufficiently convincing. The manuscript reports approximately 111.14 seconds of inference time and describes this as 8.1× real-time throughput. This statement requires much greater clarification. It is necessary to specify: the input-window duration represented by one inference; the number of samples processed per inference; whether 111.14 seconds corresponds to one window, a batch, one household day, or another unit; acquisition sampling rate; output update interval; and buffering latency.
If a 21-minute context window requires 111 seconds to process, the implementation may be faster than the input stream but still introduces a substantial output delay. That may be acceptable for periodic energy analytics, but not necessarily for “real-time” appliance detection, demand response, or rapid anomaly monitoring.
The authors should report separately: end-to-end latency; computational throughput; time to first prediction; per-sample latency; window buffering delay; preprocessing time; inference time; postprocessing time; and communication latency.
More importantly, CPU utilization reportedly approaches 100% during inference. The authors should evaluate whether the meter can concurrently maintain: metrology acquisition; harmonic analysis; display updates; tamper detection; network communication; database writing; and NILM inference.
A real-time scheduling analysis and worst-case execution-time measurement are required. The current evidence demonstrates embedded feasibility, but not necessarily responsive real-time operation.
4) “Discussion” section should be edited in a more highlighting, argumentative way. The author should analysis the reason why the tested results is achieved.
5) The literature review should be further strengthened by incorporating several recent studies related to intelligent energy management, optimization-assisted forecasting, nonlinear dynamic system analysis, and AI-driven estimation frameworks. In particular, the studies entitled “Performance Analysis of Digitally Controlled Nonlinear Systems Considering Time Delay Issues”, “A Robust Chaos-Inspired Artificial Intelligence Model for Dealing with Nonlinear Dynamics in Wind Speed Forecasting”, “A New Hybrid Model for Wind Speed Forecasting Combining Long Short-Term Memory Neural Network, Decomposition Methods and Grey Wolf Optimizer”, and “Fiscal Decentralization and SDG6 Achievement: Evidence from AI-Based Estimation for OECD Countries” provide valuable methodological perspectives on nonlinear system modeling, optimization-guided artificial intelligence, hybrid deep learning, time-series forecasting, and data-driven estimation. Although these studies address different application domains, their methodological contributions are highly relevant to the proposed edge-based smart metering architecture, particularly in terms of intelligent prediction, robust machine learning, optimization-aware model design, and AI-assisted decision support. Incorporating these studies would broaden the discussion and further strengthen the methodological background of the proposed framework.
6) The active-power reduction is reported inconsistently. The manuscript reports 71.59% reduction in one location and 92.6% relative to Raspberry Pi 4 in another. The baseline and calculation must be stated consistently.
7) Table 2 appears to contain a numerical inconsistency: 695.68 mA at 5 V corresponds to approximately 3.48 W, not 2.71 W. All current-to-power conversions should be recalculated.
8) The abstract states a hardware cost of $3–5, whereas Table 2 reports a development-board price of approximately $16. The distinction among chip cost, board cost, complete PCB cost, metrology components, power supply, enclosure, display, and communication hardware must be made explicit.
9) A complete bill of materials should be provided for the deployable meter.
10) The paper should report total system power, not only processor-board power. STPM33, display, analog front end, storage, communication interface, and power-supply losses must be included.
11) The manuscript should state whether the 0.513 W figure includes the TFT display and metrology components.
12) The term “without FPU support” should be clarified. Software-emulated floating-point preprocessing may still occur before INT8 inference.
13) The EDTCN architecture should be presented in a complete layer-by-layer table with input sizes, channels, kernel sizes, dilation factors, padding, residual connections, activations, parameters, and output dimensions.
14) The receptive-field calculation resulting in 127 timesteps should be derived explicitly.
15) The statement that 127 timesteps correspond to 21 minutes depends on a 10-second sampling interval and should be stated clearly.
16) Dataset preprocessing needs more detail, including: missing values; resampling; normalization; mains synchronization; appliance thresholds; activation filtering; train-test periods; and handling of different voltage systems.
17) The evaluation should include normalized disaggregation error and signal aggregate error in addition to MAE and F1 score.
18) The manuscript should report results separately for each appliance rather than relying mainly on dataset-level averages.
19) The relatively low F1 scores on REFIT and UK-DALE should be discussed more critically. An F1 score of 0.448 or 0.531 does not indicate uniformly strong state-detection performance.
20) The claim of “near-perfect state detection” should be removed unless supported for all relevant appliances.
21) The manuscript mentions a 30.6% precision problem but the exact experimental setting and appliance distribution producing this value should be explained.
22) The cloud-based LSTM forecasting module appears weakly connected to the edge-NILM contribution. Its data split, forecasting horizon, baseline comparison, and computational role should be stated clearly or the module should be shortened.
23) Forecasting with an MAE of 12.70 W cannot be interpreted without the scale, forecasting horizon, and baseline performance.
24) Cold-start adaptation and EWC are discussed, but the actual adaptation protocol should be described fully. The manuscript should state which parameters are updated, how much target-house data are used, and whether adaptation occurs on the F1C200s or elsewhere.
25) The claim that dishwasher MAE falls from over 100 W to approximately 1 W after adaptation is unusually large and requires detailed validation to exclude leakage or overfitting.
26) The hardware comparison should use equivalent workloads and identical peripherals. Comparing complete boards with different memory, operating systems, displays, and power states may not isolate processor efficiency.
27) Power measurement methodology should specify: measurement instrument; sampling interval; averaging period; uncertainty; input voltage stability; and whether USB/network/display activity was controlled.
28) Thermal measurements should be included during sustained inference.
29) Long-duration stability should be evaluated over at least several hours or days, including memory leakage, thermal throttling, file-system reliability, and inference drift.
30) The custom PCB should be shown clearly. The manuscript appears to refer both to a MangoPi development board and a custom-fabricated PCB, and the distinction is not consistently maintained.
31) Electrical safety, isolation distance, creepage, clearance, surge protection, and relevant smart-meter standards should be discussed before claiming industrial viability.
32) Client-side JWT routing alone does not prevent unauthorized backend access. Server-side authorization must be described and tested.
33) The statement that Stripe integration ensures PCI-DSS compliance should be moderated unless a formal compliance assessment was performed.
34) The paper should include a system failure-mode analysis covering: loss of connectivity; meter reboot; corrupted model; SPI failure; NAND corruption; power outage; and incorrect NILM predictions.
35) The source code, trained models, firmware, quantization scripts, hardware design files, and exact dataset splits should be made available in a persistent repository.
36) Several figures contain text that is too small for publication and should be redrawn with consistent technical notation.
37) The manuscript is excessively long and repetitive. Descriptions of common web technologies and generic smart-grid motivations should be condensed substantially.

Reviewer 2

Dear Authors,
I have carefully read your manuscript and found it very lengthy and lack of flow. It was difficult for me to track the main objective. Please make the manuscript concise and consider the fluency. Here are some of the points that I think need improvements:
1. Clarify the novelty against existing edge-based NILM systems. Although the manuscript claims several firsts, the distinction between the proposed NGSM and recent edge-NILM frameworks remains somewhat unclear. I encourage the authors to provide a concise comparison explicitly highlighting which technical contributions are genuinely novel, rather than relying primarily on performance comparisons.
2. Provide statistical significance for performance comparisons. The reported improvements over Seq2Point, UNet-NILM, and BERT4NILM are impressive; however, it is difficult to judge whether these improvements are statistically meaningful. Including confidence intervals or statistical significance testing across multiple experimental runs would substantially strengthen the validity of the reported performance gains.
3. Expand the description of dataset splitting and cross-dataset evaluation. The manuscript reports excellent cross-dataset generalization, but the experimental protocol should be described more explicitly. Please clarify whether the models were trained on one dataset and tested entirely on unseen datasets, or whether any fine-tuning or dataset-specific optimization was performed before evaluation.
4. Include an ablation study for each major architectural component. The proposed EDTCN incorporates several modifications, including dilated residual blocks, device-type-weighted focal loss, INT8 quantization, and compiler optimization. An ablation study quantifying the individual contribution of each component would help readers understand which innovations contribute most to the observed performance improvements.
5. Justify the choice of evaluation metrics. While MAE and F1-score are reported throughout the NILM experiments, these metrics alone may not fully characterize disaggregation performance. Including additional metrics commonly used in NILM research, such as precision, recall, SAE (Signal Aggregate Error), or energy estimation accuracy, would provide a more comprehensive evaluation.
6. Strengthen the discussion of privacy preservation. The manuscript emphasizes privacy-preserving analytics through edge inference; however, privacy is primarily discussed conceptually. I recommend providing a more rigorous analysis of the privacy guarantees offered by the proposed architecture, including potential attack scenarios, residual privacy risks, and how local inference compares with federated or encrypted approaches.

7. Discuss scalability for large-scale utility deployment. Although the proposed hardware demonstrates excellent energy efficiency, the manuscript would benefit from a discussion of how the system scales when deployed across thousands or millions of smart meters. Addressing firmware updates, remote maintenance, model synchronization, and hardware reliability would significantly improve the practical relevance of the work.
8. Validate power quality measurements against a commercial reference. The harmonic analysis is an attractive addition to the proposed system. However, since the manuscript acknowledges that THD measurements were not validated using a certified power-quality analyzer, including such validation would considerably enhance confidence in the reported measurements and their suitability for industrial deployment.
9. Clarify inference latency in practical real-time operation. The manuscript reports an inference time of approximately 111 seconds while also describing real-time edge processing. I recommend discussing how this inference latency aligns with the intended sampling interval and real-time requirements of the deployed NILM system to avoid possible confusion for readers.
10. Discuss long-term robustness under changing household behavior. The manuscript briefly mentions continual learning and domain adaptation in the literature review, yet the proposed framework appears to rely on a fixed trained model. It would be valuable to discuss how the system would maintain accuracy when appliance inventories or user behavior evolve over time.
11. Include computational complexity analysis. Given the emphasis on deployment on highly resource-constrained hardware, the manuscript would benefit from reporting computational complexity, memory footprint, parameter count, and inference operations. Such information would facilitate comparison with other lightweight NILM architectures.
12. Improve reproducibility by reporting implementation details. The manuscript contains extensive hardware descriptions; however, several implementation details necessary for reproducing the deep learning experiments remain absent. Reporting random seeds, software versions, training repetitions, optimizer settings, learning-rate schedules, and hardware specifications used during training would improve reproducibility.
13. Discuss potential limitations of cloud-assisted forecasting. The architecture performs NILM locally while offloading forecasting to the cloud. While this hybrid design is practical, the manuscript would benefit from discussing situations involving limited network connectivity, communication delays, or cloud service interruptions, and how these factors may affect overall system performance.
14. Compare hardware efficiency using standardized benchmarking metrics. The hardware comparison focuses primarily on power consumption and cost. Including additional standardized metrics such as energy per inference, throughput per watt, or inference efficiency relative to comparable embedded AI platforms would provide a more balanced evaluation of hardware performance.
15. Expand the limitations and future work section. The manuscript presents an ambitious and comprehensive framework; however, its limitations receive relatively limited discussion. A more detailed consideration of current constraints—including deployment in three-phase systems, industrial environments, larger appliance sets, different grid standards, cybersecurity considerations, and long-term field validation—would improve the scientific balance of the manuscript and help guide future research.
