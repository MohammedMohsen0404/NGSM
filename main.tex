\begin{abstract}
Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (NILM) enables appliance-level energy disaggregation from aggregate smart-meter data without per-device sensors, supporting demand response and energy-efficiency services. Existing deep-learning NILM systems, however, rely on GPU-accelerated or high-power embedded platforms (4--10~W), and cloud inference exposes high-resolution consumption data. This paper presents NGSM, an edge smart-metering system that executes NILM entirely on a low-cost embedded SoC without GPU or FPU support. NGSM deploys an Enhanced Dilated Temporal Convolutional Network (EDTCN) on an ARM926EJ-S System-in-Package (Allwinner F1C200s, 64~MB RAM). The network stacks six exponentially dilated residual blocks (259-step receptive field, 34.5~minutes at 8~s sampling) and is trained with a device-type-weighted focal loss. A controlled ablation shows that the dilated residual architecture provides the main accuracy gain, whereas the loss moves the operating point toward higher recall on burst and regular-use appliances. Post-training INT8 quantization with calibrated clipping and compiler-level fusion reduces the weight storage by a factor of 3.9 to below 1~MB with essentially unchanged regression accuracy (complete test splits). NGSM additionally performs on-device FFT-based harmonic analysis and offloads load forecasting (15-min to day-ahead) to the cloud. Under a documented chronological and household-level protocol on SustDataED2, REFIT and UK-DALE, the EDTCN reaches $5.34\pm0.10$~W, $15.38\pm0.21$~W and $15.01\pm0.15$~W MAE (F1 of 0.81, 0.46 and 0.55; mean $\pm$ std over seeds), which is 9--26\% below the strongest of three baselines (Seq2Point-, UNet-NILM- and BERT4NILM-style networks) re-implemented and trained under the identical protocol. Leave-one-house-out tests, zero-shot cross-dataset transfer and few-day adaptation with elastic weight consolidation quantify robustness to unseen households. The meter draws 0.513~W (board level) during active inference, a practical path toward scalable edge-deployed NILM.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Smart grids are evolving from automated billing toward Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) 2.0 networks~\cite{ami2.0} with distributed intelligence at the grid edge. Here, real-time means continuous acquisition, processing and exchange of measurement and control data with latencies from milliseconds (local edge processing) to a few seconds (system coordination). In NGSM the metrology, harmonic analysis and display operate at this scale, whereas the per-appliance NILM service is a near-real-time analytics layer that delivers its results within about two minutes after each 15-minute reporting interval (Section~\ref{subsubsec:latency}). The growing number of distributed energy resources increases the demand for granular monitoring at the grid edge~\cite{Market2025}, and the edge-AI market for smart grids is projected to grow by more than 24\% per year through 2034~\cite{Precedence2025}, driven by the need to process IoT energy data near its source~\cite{JISEM2025}. Many edge-oriented smart-grid studies, however, emphasize architectural decentralization without testing whether modern deep-learning models can run under realistic metering power and memory limits.

Many commercial deployments still rely on cloud-centric analytics: for high-resolution monitoring and NILM, voltage and current measurements are sent to remote servers, which adds latency and bandwidth demand, concentrates failure risk and creates an attack surface; centralized smart-grid databases are increasingly targeted by ransomware and false-data-injection attacks~\cite{EnergiesSec2025}. Fine-grained load profiles also reveal household activities, appliance usage and occupancy~\cite{MDPI2025}. Moving AI to the meter addresses these concerns but is difficult on standard microcontroller-based meters with limited processing, memory and energy budgets, and edge platforms often meet latency and memory bottlenecks on continuous high-frequency data~\cite{EdgeNILM2025}.

Lightweight networks for edge NILM generalize poorly to unseen households (cold-start and domain shift~\cite{IEEE2024}), and centralized training requires collecting user data, which motivates collaborative, privacy-preserving learning such as federated learning~\cite{SciRep2025}. A gap therefore remains for holistic solutions that jointly address the computational constraints of edge devices, adaptability across households and the privacy requirements of modern smart-grid regulation.


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Smart Metering Architectures}

The transition from traditional electromechanical meters to Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) has been driven by the need for bidirectional communication and automated billing. Early smart meters focused primarily on remote reading capabilities to reduce operational costs. However, the advent of AMI 2.0 has introduced a requirement for real-time monitoring and distributed intelligence \cite{ami2.0}. Modern architectures are now evolving beyond simple data logging to support complex grid-edge applications such as demand response and dynamic load management, requiring significantly enhanced computational capabilities comparable to industrial IoT gateways.

To address the latency and bandwidth limitations of cloud-centric models, research has shifted toward edge-centric architectures. Xu et al. proposed a distributed framework where intelligent electronic devices (IEDs) perform localized state estimation, effectively reducing the dependency on central control centers \cite{Xu2024}. Similarly, recent hardware implementations have focused on embedding high-frequency analytics directly into the meter. Schirmer et al. developed an FPGA-based smart meter capable of real-time disaggregation, demonstrating high precision but highlighting the complexity of FPGA integration \cite{Schirmer2025}. In parallel, Mallala et al. presented an IoT-based power quality analyzer for local signal processing, addressing the need for cost-effective monitoring solutions \cite{Mallala2025}. These works collectively illustrate the trend toward shifting computational workloads to the grid edge.

\subsection{Data Analytics and AI in Smart Grids}

\subsubsection{Harmonics and Power Quality Analysis}
The detection of power quality disturbances has traditionally relied on specialized, high-cost analyzers. However, recent initiatives have sought to integrate these capabilities into standard smart meters. Moustafa et al. demonstrated a web-based power quality and energy monitoring system built on a Raspberry Pi and a power quality front-end that detects disturbances such as sags, swells, interruptions and voltage imbalance \cite{Moustafa2025PQ}. While effective, such implementations often employ single-board computers with relatively high power consumption, creating a need for solutions that provide equivalent spectral analysis on power-optimized industrial platforms. Harmonic measurement methods and their accuracy classes are standardized in IEC 61000-4-7 \cite{iec61000_4_7}, which embedded implementations have to follow for their indices to be comparable with those of dedicated instruments.

\subsubsection{Energy Demand Forecasting}
Short-term load forecasting (STLF) is critical for grid stability, and deep learning has largely replaced statistical methods for capturing non-linear temporal dependencies. Hybrid LSTM--Transformer \cite{HybridLSTMTrans2025} and LSTM--XGBoost ensembles \cite{HybridLSTMXGB2025} have been proposed for demand forecasting, and signal decomposition combined with optimizer-tuned LSTM networks captures non-linear wind-speed dynamics \cite{Altan2021wind, Baris2024wind}. Studies of digitally controlled nonlinear systems \cite{Yanarates2023delay} and metaheuristically tuned BiLSTM frameworks \cite{Avci2026sdg6} underline that a digital measurement and prediction chain should be evaluated against its delays and validated by multi-horizon comparison, as is done for the forecasting benchmark and the latency analysis of this paper. The Temporal Fusion Transformer addresses multi-horizon forecasting with known future inputs \cite{Lim2021tft}, and histogram-based gradient-boosted trees \cite{Ke2017lightgbm} are an inexpensive alternative when lag and calendar features are supplied. These models often incur significant computational overhead, so predictive performance must be balanced against resource use in edge deployment.

\subsubsection{Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (Deep Learning)}
In the domain of Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (NILM), deep neural networks have surpassed combinatorial optimization methods. Convolutional architectures like Sequence-to-Point (Seq2Point) have proven effective in mapping aggregate power windows to appliance activations \cite{Zhang2018}. More recently, Transformer-based models such as BERT4NILM \cite{Yue2020}, NILMFormer \cite{Sykiotis2024}, and ELECTRIcity \cite{Massidda2022} have been adapted to model long-term temporal dependencies in energy data. While achieving state-of-the-art accuracy, these models typically fail to meet the strict memory and latency constraints of embedded smart meters, often requiring GPU acceleration for efficient inference.

\subsubsection{Edge Deployment of NILM Models}
The practical deployment of deep learning-based NILM on resource-constrained hardware remains an underexplored challenge. Recent benchmarking studies have evaluated NILM algorithms on standard edge platforms such as the Raspberry Pi 4, demonstrating feasible inference times but with power consumption in the range of 4 to 7 Watts \cite{EdgeNILM2025}. To achieve sub-second inference latency, other approaches rely on dedicated AI accelerators such as the NVIDIA Jetson Nano, which provides GPU-accelerated inference for complex architectures but at a cost exceeding \$99 and power consumption of 5 to 10 Watts \cite{Xu2024VMD}. These power envelopes and unit costs render such platforms unsuitable for mass-scale utility deployment, where millions of meters must operate continuously within stringent energy budgets. The gap between laboratory-grade prototyping hardware and industrially viable, sub-Watt (< 1W) embedded solutions capable of executing deep learning inference remains a critical barrier to the widespread adoption of intelligent energy disaggregation.

\subsubsection{Privacy and Adaptation in NILM}
The centralization of high-resolution energy data raises significant privacy concerns, as fine-grained load profiles can reveal sensitive household activities \cite{MDPI2025}. Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a solution to enable collaborative training without sharing raw data \cite{Li2024, SciRep2025}. However, privacy alone does not ensure long-term reliability; models must also adapt to changing household environments. Techniques such as Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) have been proposed to support continual learning \cite{Kirkpatrick2017}, yet few studies have successfully integrated these adaptive mechanisms into a cohesive, resource-constrained metering system covering the entire pipeline from signal acquisition to inference.

\subsection{Contribution and Positioning}
\label{subsec:contribution}

A gap remains in unifying hardware efficiency, advanced AI and robustness across households: many solutions rely on power-hungry prototyping hardware (e.g., Raspberry Pi 4) or on low-power microcontrollers that cannot run complex NILM inference \cite{EdgeNILM2025}. This work deploys a dilated temporal convolutional network on an ARM926EJ-S System-in-Package without FPU, GPU or accelerator, at 0.513~W board-level power during active inference.

This work introduces the Next-Generation Smart Grid Meter (NGSM), a comprehensive framework that bridges this gap. Unlike previous prototypes, the NGSM is built on a custom SiP-based architecture (Allwinner F1C200s), achieving significant reductions in active power consumption. It integrates an Enhanced Dilated Temporal Convolutional Network (EDTCN) for NILM with device-type-weighted focal loss, achieving lower error than baselines re-implemented under an identical protocol on three public datasets (SustDataED2, REFIT, UK-DALE), together with post-training INT8 quantization for embedded execution. Relative to the work in Table~\ref{tab:qualitative_comparison}, the technical contributions are: (i) \textit{Deployment}: a dilated residual network quantized to true INT8 with calibrated clipping and run on a single ARM926EJ-S core without FPU or accelerator, with weights of 0.72--0.98~MB, whereas the cited edge-NILM benchmarks use a Raspberry Pi 4 (4--7~W) or a Jetson Nano (5--10~W) \cite{EdgeNILM2025, Xu2024VMD}; (ii) \textit{Model attribution}: a controlled ablation attributing the accuracy gain to the dilated residual architecture (259-step receptive field) and showing what the device-type-weighted loss does and does not contribute; (iii) \textit{Evaluation}: per-appliance, multi-metric results under a documented protocol, with unseen-household, cross-dataset and few-day adaptation experiments; (iv) \textit{System}: metrology, harmonic analysis, appliance-level analytics, a forecasting service and user applications in one meter design. The contributions are supported by measurements on three fronts. On hardware, the custom SiP design reduces the active power to 102.68~mA at 5~V (0.513~W), which is 71.6\% below the second-generation Raspberry Pi Zero 2W prototype and 85.3\% below the first-generation Raspberry Pi 4 prototype (Table~\ref{tab:hardwarebenchmarks}), for a prototype bill of materials of USD~117.95 at single-unit prices (Table~\ref{tab:bom}). On analytics, the EDTCN-NILM reaches $5.34\pm0.10$~W MAE (F1 0.814) on SustDataED2, $15.38\pm0.21$~W (F1 0.464) on REFIT and $15.01\pm0.15$~W (F1 0.549) on UK-DALE, 9--26\% below the strongest of three baselines trained under the identical protocol; a controlled ablation attributes the gain mainly to the dilated residual architecture, and the study reports every appliance with SAE, NDE, precision, recall and F1 alongside MAE, including the false-positive behavior of regression-based NILM (30.6\% pooled precision on REFIT at 15~W). Harmonic analysis follows IEC 61000-4-7 and is verified on synthetic signals but not yet against a certified analyzer, and the cloud forecasting service is 7--26\% below the strongest of four baselines (15-min MAE 121~W, day-ahead 164~W on the UK-DALE test year). On the F1C200s (ARM926EJ-S, 64~MB RAM, no FPU or accelerator), INT8 quantization reduces the weight storage 3.9$\times$ to below 1~MB with regression MAE within 3\% of FP32, and one 68-minute window is processed in 101--123~s (33--40$\times$ faster than the data rate, 11--14\% CPU duty cycle at a 15-minute cadence) with 66.77\% memory utilization.


\section{NGSM System Architecture}
\label{sec:system_arch}

The proposed Next-Generation Smart Grid Meter (NGSM) functions as a comprehensive Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) 2.0 solution, designed to facilitate bidirectional communication and distributed processing. This architecture fundamentally shifts the computational load from centralized cloud servers to the edge of the grid, addressing the latency and scalability challenges inherent in traditional cloud-centric monitoring \cite{MDPI_NILM2025, ResearchGate2025}. The system topology is delineated into three primary domains: the End-User (Edge Node), the Communication Network (WAN), and the Head-End System (HES). As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:system_arch}, this distributed approach enables real-time responsiveness and reduces bandwidth congestion by processing high-frequency electrical data locally.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/img1.png}
    \caption{High-level architecture of the NGSM system illustrating the Edge-Cloud data flow.}
    \label{fig:system_arch}
\end{figure}

The fundamental unit of the architecture is the smart meter, which operates as an embedded Linux device integrating software and hardware within a System on Chip (SoC) architecture to support AI-integrated sustainable energy management \cite{ResearchGate2025}. The internal hardware architecture is composed of five critical functional blocks. First, the Analog Front End (AFE) is responsible for the high-frequency acquisition of voltage and current signals, performing Digital Signal Processing (DSP) and electricity tampering detection through live-neutral current comparison \cite{Energize2025}. Second, the Single Board Computer (SBC) hosts the primary SoC, storage, and communication modems, serving as the central processing unit capable of supporting complex AI models. These core elements are supported by a User Interaction module managing the TFT display, a Power Supply unit for regulation, and an Enclosure Tampering Detection system that utilizes electromechanical limit switches to trigger immediate interrupts upon unauthorized disassembly, a critical defense against physical attacks \cite{IJNSA2025}.

Data transmission between the edge nodes and the central infrastructure is mediated by a Wide Area Network (WAN) connecting to the Head-End System (HES). The HES acts as the centralized aggregation point, comprising a web-based dashboard and a smartphone mobile application. Beyond simple data logging, the HES executes high-level administrative tasks including time-of-use (ToU) billing calculation and secure remote payments. Furthermore, it handles advanced visualization by presenting hourly consumption data and appliance-level breakdowns to the consumer, a capability essential for modern utility customer engagement \cite{EY2025}. The HES also serves as the repository for Appliance Detection Analytics, receiving pre-processed classification results from the edge nodes. This strategy of transmitting only inference results rather than raw high-frequency waveforms significantly optimizes network bandwidth and reduces the processing latency typically associated with edge-cloud transfers \cite{MDPI_NILM2025}.


\section{Hardware Implementation}
\label{sec:hardware}

The development of the NGSM hardware followed an iterative engineering methodology, evolving through three distinct generations. This progression was driven by the necessity to balance computational performance for edge-AI tasks against the constraints of power consumption, thermal management, and mass-production viability.

\subsection{First Generation: Proof of Concept}
\label{subsec:gen1}

The first generation validated the embedded Linux software stack and the metrology algorithms without power or form-factor constraints. Prototyping started on a Raspberry Pi 3 Model B \cite{pi3}, and the benchmarks reported here were run on a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B \cite{pi4} (quad-core Cortex-A72 at 1.8~GHz, 8~GB LPDDR4) as a high-performance baseline. The single board computer coordinates signal acquisition and the user interface (Figure~\ref{fig:gen1_block}).


Sensing used discrete modules: a ZMPT101B voltage sensor \cite{ZMPT101B} (1000:1000 micro-voltage transformer) and an SCT-013 split-core current transformer \cite{SCT013401} (up to 100~A, about 3\% linearity error). The transformer ratio is given by Equation~\ref{eq:transformer_ratio}.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transformer_ratio}
\frac{I_{secondary}}{I_{primary}} = \frac{N_{primary}}{N_{secondary}}
\end{equation}
Because the SCT-013-100 has no internal burden resistor, an external 33~$\Omega$ resistor converts the secondary current to a voltage for the 16-bit ADS1115 converter on the I2C bus, with the peak voltage given by Equation~\ref{eq:peak_voltage}.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:peak_voltage}
V_{peak} = \sqrt{2} \times I_{RMS(secondary)} \times R_{burden}
\end{equation}
The prototype was validated under active load, confirming end-to-end sensing and data flow, but its high idle current (542~mA) and unit cost motivated the next generation.

\subsection{Second Generation: Embedded Optimization and Integrated Metrology}
\label{subsec:gen2}

The second generation addressed the high idle current (542~mA) and the calibration instability of discrete sensors by using a Raspberry Pi Zero 2W \cite{RPiZeroW_Datasheet} (quad-core Cortex-A53 at 1~GHz, 512~MB, onboard Wi-Fi and Bluetooth), which lowers the idle current by 75\% to 134~mA, and the STMicroelectronics STPM33 \cite{STPM33_Datasheet} metering ASSP (Figure~\ref{fig:gen2_hardware}).


The STPM33 computes active, reactive and apparent energy in hardware and connects over SPI. Voltage is sensed through a resistive divider (810~k$\Omega$ and 470~$\Omega$) with a 22~nF anti-aliasing capacitor, and current through CTs of 2.4~mV/A with 10~nF capacitors and 1~k$\Omega$ resistors \cite{EVALSTPM33_UserManual}; the connection topology with a dual-phase phantom load is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:gen2_connection}.


Load tests on the integrated TFT panel showed a noise floor of 0.014~A without load (Figure~\ref{fig:gen2_idle}) and a correct reading of 0.431~A at 227.94~V against the phantom load (Figure~\ref{fig:gen2_load}); the final assembly is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:gen2_final}.




\subsection{Third Generation: Custom Fabrication and Path to Scalability}
\label{subsec:gen3}

The third generation moves the system from a functional prototype toward a lower-power, lower-cost and more compact design based on a custom double-layer PCB around the Allwinner F1C200s SoC \cite{f1c200s}; it remains an uncertified proof-of-concept prototype (see the paragraph on prototype status below).

The F1C200s (on the MangoPi R3 board \cite{mangopi}) has an ARM926EJ-S core at 420~MHz and 64~MB of DDR1 inside the package, which removes external RAM and simplifies the layout. Compared with the Raspberry Pi Zero of the second generation it uses much less power, although the board is not cheaper at single-unit prices (USD~19.60 versus 15, Table~\ref{tab:hardwarebenchmarks}), and it has a single SPI bus. The STPM33 (SPI mode 3) and the TFT display (mode 0) interfere on a shared bus even with the idle chip select disabled; a demultiplexer and a tri-state buffer were tried, and a software SPI was finally used because of a hardware issue in the demultiplexer used, cost reduction and PCB space.

Storage was migrated from removable SD cards to an onboard SPI NAND flash to avoid contact failures and file-system corruption. The Linux system was built with Buildroot~\cite{buildroot}; the Device Tree disables the LCD controller, TV encoder and camera interface, dedicates the hardware SPI1 controller on Port E to the STPM33, and enables USB OTG with RNDIS Ethernet gadget mode for local SSH maintenance. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:firmware_stack}, the monolithic kernel with built-in drivers is separated from user-space daemons, and inter-process communication decouples acquisition from visualization and network tasks so that failures in non-critical services do not interrupt metrology.

The layout keeps the AC inputs and the low-voltage digital signals in separate regions to preserve signal integrity for the STPM33 analog front end; creepage and clearance distances were not verified against a standard. The custom PCB is a carrier board with the MangoPi board (F1C200s) soldered onto it, the STPM33 interface, the TFT connector, the tamper switch, the buttons and indicator LED, and the Hi-Link AC/DC converter (Figures~\ref{fig:gen3_pcb_schematic} and \ref{fig:gen3_pcb_3d}). Power and latency were measured on all three generations; for the third they were measured on the custom PCB with the MangoPi soldered onto it, not on the MangoPi alone.

\paragraph{Prototype status and measurement conditions} The current implementation is a functional proof of concept (PoC) that validates the core architecture and measurement algorithms; it is an uncertified benchtop prototype. Creepage and clearance distances, surge testing per IEC 61000-4-5, certification under IEC 62052-11 and IEC 62053-21, and formal thermal and environmental stress tests were not carried out and remain future work. Power was measured with a UNI-T UT61A digital multimeter at the 5~V supply input of each generation, with all hardware connected in the third generation, so the values carry the accuracy of that instrument and no averaging period applies. The 24-hour soak test lasted more than 24~hours with all functions running and readings uploaded to a head-end system whose receiving server was inactive; quantitative logs of memory use and inference time over the run, and the power at the mains side of the AC/DC converter, are not reported. To reduce storage I/O, inter-process communication was migrated from file-based exchange to message queues and shared memory.

\section{Software Components}
\label{sec:software}

\subsection{Web-Based System Architecture}
\label{subsec:web_arch}

NGSM meters connect through a Wide Area Network (WAN) to a centralized Head-End System (HES) that hosts appliance-detection analytics, consumption forecasting and payment processing (Figure \ref{fig:system_block}). Visualization is decoupled from metrological acquisition, so that heavy query loads from user applications do not degrade the real-time metering tasks of the edge node \cite{8849012, Bera2014}.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/img12.png}
    \caption{NGSM System Block Diagram illustrating the data flow between Edge Nodes, WAN, and the Head-End System services.}
    \label{fig:system_block}
\end{figure}

The software stack (Figure \ref{fig:web_arch}) follows a service-oriented architecture. The backend exposes a Meter API (raw voltage and current payloads), a Consumption API (historical aggregation), an Event API (high-priority events such as tampering alerts), a Payment API integrated with the Stripe gateway and a Bill API that generates invoices from the consumption records stored in an MSSQL database; the communication layer was designed for the latency and packet loss typical of smart-grid networks \cite{Patel2024}.


On the meter, the local web server is lighttpd, chosen for its small memory footprint \cite{Babovic2016}, and the communication layer is implemented in C++ with the gSOAP toolkit for SOAP/REST web services \cite{Engelen2002}, so that serialization overhead does not interfere with the high-frequency sampling of the metrology thread.

\subsection{Client-Side Web Application}
\label{subsec:webapp}

The consumer-facing component of the HES is a React single-page application hosted on Microsoft Azure; its component-based design suits live dashboards \cite{Chopra2021_FlaskReactDashboard}.

The routing logic of the application (Figure \ref{fig:user_flow}) distinguishes between public ``landing'' zones and private routes, and a client-side handler that checks the JSON Web Token (JWT) only controls what the interface displays; it is not access control; authorization is enforced on the server (details in the Supplementary Material (Privacy and security details)).


A built-in FAQ module with an accordion layout answers common questions on billing cycles, meter installation and account management and reduces the number of support tickets.

The Consumption Dashboard (Figure \ref{fig:consumption_page}) retrieves historical usage from the cloud Consumption API for user-defined date ranges and renders interactive bar charts and numerical summaries of consumption and cost; interactive visualizations of this kind support behavioral change by turning kilowatt-hours into financial insight \cite{mti3030056}.


Financial settlement is handled by a Payment and Billing module that integrates the Stripe payment gateway API. The module displays the outstanding balance and billing period, and payment confirmations are reflected in the account status through callback hooks from the Payment API.

\subsection{Cross-Platform Mobile Application}
\label{subsec:mobileapp}

A Flutter/Dart mobile application gives users access to their energy data on Android and iOS from a single codebase \cite{Gülcüoğlu2021}; all data requests are authenticated against the HES APIs before sensitive consumption or billing information is displayed (Figure \ref{fig:mobile_activity}).


Users log in with their web credentials and can reset a forgotten password without administrative intervention.

An analytics module toggles between energy in kilowatt-hours and the corresponding cost (Figures \ref{fig:mobile_kwh} and \ref{fig:mobile_cost}); mobile feedback of this kind promotes energy conservation \cite{chatzigeorgiou2021}.



The billing module shows a digital invoice for the current cycle and the history of previous invoices.

A distinguishing feature of the NGSM mobile application is the near-real-time visualization of Appliance Detection results. Leveraging the Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (NILM) analytics processed by the edge node, the application displays a status dashboard for major household appliances that is refreshed with every 15-minute reporting interval (Section~\ref{subsubsec:latency}). Each detected device is represented with its operational state (ON/OFF) and estimated power draw in the most recent interval. This granular visibility empowers consumers to identify and isolate high-consumption devices, bridging the gap between aggregate meter readings and specific appliance usage.

\section{Advanced Data Analytics and ML/DL Techniques}
\label{sec:ai}

This section presents the advanced analytics capabilities integrated into NGSM, focusing on algorithms that can be executed close to the measurement source under the latency, privacy, and resource constraints of smart metering deployments. Building on the embedded hardware and software stack described earlier, the proposed analytics pipeline performs local signal analysis for power-quality monitoring and supports intelligent load understanding through Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (NILM). In addition to real-time edge operation, the section introduces a training and adaptation methodology that enables robust performance across heterogeneous households while preserving data locality through privacy-aware learning mechanisms.


\subsection{Power Quality and Harmonic Analysis}
\label{subsec:harmonics}

Power quality is a fundamental indicator of grid stability: non-linear loads such as switch-mode power supplies and variable-frequency drives draw non-sinusoidal currents \cite{dugan2012electrical}, causing overheating, neutral-conductor overloading and transformer resonance, so early detection \cite{eristi2022new} is critical at the grid edge. A periodic non-sinusoidal signal is the sum of a DC component, the fundamental and its harmonics (Equation~\ref{eq:fourier}):

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fourier}
y(t) = Y_0 + \sum_{h=1}^{\infty} Y_h \sqrt{2} \sin(h\omega t - \phi_h)
\end{equation}

where $Y_0$ is the DC component, $Y_h$ the RMS value of harmonic $h$, $\omega$ the fundamental angular frequency and $\phi_h$ the phase displacement \cite{wakileh2001power}. The Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) is the ratio of the RMS value of all harmonics to that of the fundamental (Equation~\ref{eq:thd}):

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:thd}
THD = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_{h=2}^{H} Y_h^2}}{Y_1}
\end{equation}

with $H = 40$, the harmonic range of IEC 61000-4-7 \cite{iec61000_4_7}.

The spectral composition of voltage and current is computed locally at the meter node \cite{shang2021achieving} with the harmonic-subgroup method of IEC 61000-4-7 \cite{iec61000_4_7}. The fundamental period is tracked from the positive-going zero crossings of the band-pass-filtered voltage, and both signals are resampled synchronously so that each window contains exactly ten fundamental cycles (200~ms at 50~Hz) with 1024 points per cycle. A rectangular-window DFT places every harmonic on a 5~Hz bin, $Y_h$ is the RMS value of the harmonic subgroup (the bin at $h f_1$ and its two neighbours), and window values are aggregated as RMS over consecutive windows, as in IEC 61000-4-30 \cite{iec61000_4_30}. Synchronous windowing avoids the spectral leakage of a fixed-length FFT with peak picking, whose accuracy depends on how closely the record length matches an integer number of grid cycles. The analysis runs on the meter and the indices are reported at the 10-min interval; its execution time and its share of the single core during inference have not been measured separately (see the limitations).

The estimator was verified on synthetic voltage and current signals with known harmonic content (seven profiles from 6.2\% to 110\% THD, fundamental frequencies from 49 to 51~Hz, a frequency drift, an interharmonic and the measured noise level; the test design, Table~\ref{tab:thd_verification} and Figure~\ref{fig:thd_verification} are in the Supplementary Material). Compared with a fixed-length Blackman--Harris FFT with peak picking and with a notch method (which estimates THD plus noise), the subgroup estimator stays within 0.31 percentage points (pp) of the true THD for the profile matching the measured current and within 1.09~pp in the worst case (the low-distortion profile at the measured noise floor), whereas peak picking deviates by up to 5.4~pp at 50.2~Hz and 10.9~pp under frequency drift.



The method was applied to a 5-s capture from the prototype acquisition chain (220{,}000 samples at approximately 44~kHz) with a 47~W non-linear load of power factor 0.84 and a fundamental frequency of $50.13 \pm 0.01$~Hz. Figure~\ref{fig:thd_analysis} shows the waveforms and the harmonic spectra of orders 2 to 15 with their expanded uncertainty ($k = 2$). The current is dominated by the 3rd (31.5\%), 5th (10.1\%) and 9th (5.1\%) harmonics, characteristic of a capacitor-input rectifier, and the largest voltage harmonic is the 3rd at 1.3\%. Three cross-checks independent of the FFT implementation (Table~\ref{tab:thd_consistency} in the Supplementary Material) agree: a least-squares fit in the manner of IEEE Std 1057 \cite{ieee1057} within 0.003~pp for $THD_V$ and 0.37~pp for $THD_I$, the time-domain and per-harmonic active powers within 0.02\%, and an in-band current noise (13.0~mA) that originates in the analog front end, not in the converter (0.53~mA).

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/thd_analysis_iec.png}
    \caption{Waveforms and harmonic spectra of the prototype capture.}
    \label{fig:thd_analysis}
\end{figure}

The uncertainty was evaluated following the GUM \cite{jcgm100} with Monte Carlo propagation \cite{jcgm101} (budget in Table~\ref{tab:thd_uncertainty} in the Supplementary Material), giving $THD_V = 2.15 \pm 0.03\%$ and $THD_I = 35.02 \pm 0.84\%$ (expanded uncertainty, $k = 2$; repeatability dominates the current uncertainty). These prototype-level values for the 47~W load were verified on synthetic signals and cross-checked with independent estimators, but they have not been compared with a certified or commercial power-quality analyzer, and the prototype is not certified for compliance assessment; the current THD is reported as an appliance-level indicator of load-induced distortion.

\subsection{Energy Demand Forecasting}
\label{subsec:forecasting}

NGSM complements the on-meter analytics with a cloud forecasting service for the aggregate household demand. Energy demand forecasting enables cost-aware and carbon-aware operation of smart grids \cite{Alsamraee2025}; statistical models such as ARIMA are routinely compared with deep recurrent networks \cite{Hossain2025}, and household demand is particularly hard to predict because of non-stationary usage and behavioural variability. The service consumes the 15-min interval values that the meter already reports for billing and NILM, so it needs no additional data, and provides forecasts of up to 1~h (e.g., for demand response) and day-ahead forecasts for planning. This section describes the benchmark used to select the models, their computational cost and the behaviour of the service when the connection to the cloud is lost.

All experiments use the aggregate active power of House~1 of UK-DALE \cite{Kelly2015ukdale}, recorded every 6~s from November 2012 to April 2017; the target is the mean active power of each 15-min interval, the reporting interval of the meter. An interval is scored only if at least 80\% of its 6-s samples are present, missing intervals are never interpolated, and model inputs carry forward the last available value so that no future information enters a forecast. A resolution finer than the native 6~s is not meaningful (after resampling to 1~s, 85\% of the points are interpolated and one-second persistence already reaches 4.3~W MAE). The data are split chronologically into whole years (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_protocol}): training April 2013--March 2015, validation April 2015--March 2016, testing April 2016--March 2017; earlier months are excluded because 29--39\% of their intervals are missing. Every interval whose 24-h horizon stays inside its split is a forecast origin; each model forecasts the next 1--96 intervals, and all models are scored on identical origins and targets (34{,}944 origins in the test year).


Four reference forecasts serve as baselines: persistence, seasonal naive forecasts repeating the value of one day or one week earlier, and the ARIMA(1,1,0) model of the original NGSM design fitted on the training years. They are compared with gradient-boosted regression trees (GBM) \cite{Ke2017lightgbm, Pedregosa2011sklearn} (one model per reported horizon), the stacked LSTM of the NGSM design (hidden sizes 64, 32 and 16; two days of input, all 96 horizons at once) and a Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) \cite{Lim2021tft} implemented with Darts \cite{Herzen2022darts}. The LSTM and TFT are trained with the mean squared error on min--max scaled data, excluding unscored intervals, with early stopping on the validation year, and every learned model uses five seeds. Features and hyper-parameters are listed in the Supplementary Material (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_repro}).

Accuracy is reported at horizons of 15~min, 1~h, 4~h and 24~h as MAE, RMSE and normalized MAE (nMAE, MAE divided by the mean test-year load of 396~W), as mean and standard deviation over the five seeds for the learned models. Skill is the MAE reduction relative to the strongest baseline at each horizon. Differences are tested with the Diebold--Mariano test \cite{Diebold1995} on the absolute errors of the seed-averaged forecasts, with a Newey--West variance estimate \cite{Newey1987} whose lag covers at least one day.

Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} and Figure~\ref{fig:forecast_horizon} give the test-year results. From 1~h ahead, all three learned models beat the strongest baseline by 16--26\% (Diebold--Mariano $p<0.001$). At 15~min the last observed value is already a strong predictor: only the GBM improves on persistence and ARIMA(1,1,0), by 7\%, the LSTM is on par (mean MAE 0.6\% higher), and the TFT is 15\% less accurate and varies strongly between seeds. The GBM is the most accurate and most stable model up to 1~h (the LSTM and TFT are 2.8\% and 5.5\% worse, $p<0.001$), the TFT and GBM are equivalent at 4~h (0.4\% difference, $p=0.11$), and the TFT is the most accurate day-ahead (2.1\% and 3.9\% lower error than the GBM and LSTM, $p\le0.001$). NGSM therefore uses the GBM up to 1~h and the TFT for day-ahead forecasts. The nMAE of 31--41\% reflects the limited predictability of a single household at 15-min resolution, where individual appliance switching dominates the load.

Forecasting runs in the cloud because training requires years of history and, for the TFT, a GPU, whereas the meter only reports the 15-min values it already produces (768 bytes per day); a job scheduled every 15~min on cloud CPUs returns updated forecasts to each meter, so the meter spends no processing time or energy on forecasting. Inference is lightweight (Table~\ref{tab:forecast_compute} in the Supplementary Material): on one CPU thread a forecast takes 5.4~ms per horizon for the GBM, 2.1~ms for all 96 horizons for the LSTM and 74~ms for the TFT, so one core can refresh the 15-min and 1-h GBM forecasts of about 83{,}000 meters or the day-ahead TFT forecasts of more than 12{,}000 meters every 15~min.

Because metrology, harmonic analysis and NILM run on the meter, a loss of connectivity affects only the forecasts. During an outage the meter buffers its 15-min values and uploads them on reconnection, so no data are lost, and it keeps the most recent 24-h cloud forecast; after $k$ intervals without connection, the cached forecast for the next hour is $k + 4$ intervals old. As a local alternative it can compute persistence, seasonal naive or ARIMA(1,1,0) forecasts. A communication delay of up to one interval corresponds to the 15-min row of Table~\ref{tab:forecast_outage}.



\subsection{Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring}
\label{subsec:nilm}

Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (NILM) enables appliance-level energy disaggregation from aggregate power measurements without per-device sensors. This subsection presents a NILM framework based on an Enhanced Dilated Temporal Convolutional Network (EDTCN), evaluated on three public datasets under a documented protocol and optimized for embedded deployment.

\textbf{Task Formulation:} The framework addresses two related tasks: (1) \textit{Regression} -- estimating the continuous power of every appliance in Watts (evaluated with MAE, RMSE, SAE, NDE and energy accuracy), and (2) \textit{State detection} -- deciding whether each appliance is ON (evaluated with precision, recall and F1). The regression network has one output head per appliance and is trained with a regression loss only; there is no classification term in the regression objective. ON/OFF states are obtained by thresholding the estimated power at 15~W (the same threshold is applied to the ground truth). A separate classification network with the same backbone is trained with a weighted binary cross-entropy for the comparison in Section~\ref{subsubsec:classification}. All F1, precision and recall values in this paper are computed from the regression outputs unless explicitly labeled as ``Classification Model'' results.

\subsubsection{Enhanced EDTCN architecture}
\label{subsubsec:dtcn_architecture}

The proposed architecture extends the baseline Temporal Convolutional Network with deeper layers, wider channels, and exponentially increasing dilation factors to capture long-range temporal dependencies in power consumption patterns. The model consists of three primary components: input projection, stacked residual TCN blocks, and per-appliance output heads.

The core dilated convolution operation is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dilated_conv}
F(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \cdot x_{t - d \cdot k},
\end{equation}

where $K$ is the kernel size, $w_k$ are the convolution weights, and $d$ is the dilation factor. Each residual TCN block applies two dilated convolutions (kernel size $K=3$, dilation $d_i$, padding $(K-1)d_i$, output trimmed to the input length), each followed by batch normalization, ReLU and dropout ($p=0.2$), and adds the block input through an identity (or $1{\times}1$) skip connection. Six blocks with exponentially increasing dilations $d_i\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}$ follow an input projection ($\mathrm{Conv1d}_{1\rightarrow128}$, kernel 7). A dilated convolution with kernel $K$ and dilation $d$ extends the receptive field by $(K-1)d$ steps, so the two convolutions of block $i$ add $2(K-1)d_i$ steps and the receptive field of the complete network is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rf}
R = 1+(k_{in}-1)+2(K-1)\sum_{i=1}^{6} d_i = 1+6+2\cdot 2\cdot 63 = 259\ \text{steps},
\end{equation}
as derived block by block in Table~\ref{tab:rf}. All three datasets are resampled to $\Delta t=8$~s (Section~\ref{subsubsec:datasets}), so $R\,\Delta t = 2072$~s $=34.5$ minutes of history, and the 512-step input window spans 68.3 minutes. For another sampling interval the span scales linearly with $\Delta t$ (4.3 minutes at 1~s, 25.9 minutes at 6~s, 43.2 minutes at 10~s).


The model has 128 channels and 706,889 trainable parameters in the 9-appliance REFIT configuration (743,948 in the 12-appliance SustDataED2 and UK-DALE configurations). The input projection contributes 1,280 parameters (including batch normalization), each residual block 99,072 and each appliance head 12,353. One forward pass over a 512-step window requires 377.8 M multiply--accumulate operations (MACs) for 9 heads (396.7 M for 12 heads); the FP32 weights occupy 2.83~MB and the INT8 weights 0.71~MB. The same configuration is used for all datasets, only the number of output heads changes. Figure~\ref{fig:dtcn_arch} gives an overview of the architecture and Table~\ref{tab:layers} lists every layer.


Given an aggregate sequence $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times1\times T}$ with $T=512$, the input projection, the residual blocks and the heads compute
\begin{align*}
\mathbf{h}_0&=\mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{BN}(\mathrm{Conv1d}_{1\rightarrow128}(\mathbf{x})))),\\
\mathbf{h}_i&=\mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(F_i(\mathbf{h}_{i-1})+\mathbf{h}_{i-1})),\\
\mathbf{y}_a&=\mathrm{Conv1d}_{32\rightarrow1}(\mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{Conv1d}_{128\rightarrow32}(\mathbf{h}_6)))),
\end{align*}
with $F_i$ the two dilated convolution--normalization stages, $A$ heads mapping the shared representation $\mathbf{h}_6$ to the per-appliance power $\mathbf{y}_a$, so that the output is $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times A}$. The shared trunk learns temporal features common to all appliances (cycles, transients, baseline variations) and the independent heads specialize in appliance-specific signatures. The exponential dilation schedule realizes a hierarchy of temporal scales: shallow blocks resolve start-up transients, deep blocks aggregate cycle-level context such as washing-machine program phases.

\subsubsection{Training Strategy and Loss Functions}
\label{subsubsec:training_strategy}

The framework employs two specialized models trained independently: a regression model for continuous power prediction and a classification model for binary ON/OFF state detection. This separation addresses the inherent conflict between minimizing power estimation error (MAE) and maximizing state detection accuracy (F1-score).

\paragraph{Regression Model} The regression model predicts per-appliance power with a device-type-weighted focal loss that targets three NILM difficulties: appliance power scales spanning two orders of magnitude (2~W laptops versus 2000~W kettles), temporal imbalance (burst devices are active only a few percent of the time), and the need to concentrate learning on samples with large errors. With the absolute error $e_{i,t,a}=|\hat{y}_{i,t,a}-y_{i,t,a}|$ and the normalized error $p_{i,t,a}=\min\!\big(1,\,e_{i,t,a}/\max(|y_{i,t,a}|,\,y_{\min})\big)$ with $y_{\min}=1$~W, the loss is 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:focal_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{focal} = \frac{1}{N\,T\,A}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{a=1}^{A} w_{\tau(a)}\; p_{i,t,a}^{\,\gamma}\,\Big(\alpha\, e_{i,t,a}^{2} + (1-\alpha)\, e_{i,t,a}\Big),
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the batch size, $T=512$ the window length, $A$ the number of appliances, $\tau(a)\in\{A,B,C\}$ the usage type of appliance $a$, $w_\tau$ its weight, $\gamma$ the focusing exponent and $\alpha$ the weight of the squared-error term. The floor $y_{\min}$ and the clipping to 1 bound the modulation factor $p^{\gamma}$ to $[0,1]$ in every appliance state; it is the regression analogue of the $(1-p_t)^{\gamma}$ factor of classification focal loss, down-weighting samples that are already predicted well. The loss is a pure regression objective with one head per appliance (Section~\ref{subsubsec:dtcn_architecture}); the quadratic--linear mix combines the strong penalty of MSE on high-power events with the outlier robustness of MAE. It is averaged over batch, time and appliances and contains no threshold; the 15~W ON/OFF threshold is only used for state metrics.

The type weights are fixed priors, not learned: $w_A=w_B=1.5$ for always-on/cycling (type A) and regular-use (type B) appliances, and $w_C=2.0$ for burst appliances (type C), i.e. larger weights for appliances that are active more rarely and more abruptly, with $\alpha=0.7$ and $\gamma=1.0$ (``Config 3''). Three configurations differing in $\gamma$ and in the weights were compared on REFIT and yielded MAE values of 15.66, 16.09 and 17.24~W (Config 3, 2 and 1), i.e. a spread of at most 1.6~W, showing that the model is not sensitive to these choices within the tested range. The same setting is used on all datasets without per-dataset tuning. The contribution of the loss relative to MSE, MAE, standard focal loss and type-weighted MSE is quantified in the ablation study of Section~\ref{subsubsec:ablation}.

\paragraph{Classification Model} The classification head predicts binary ON/OFF states using weighted binary cross-entropy:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bce_loss}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_{BCE} &= -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{a=1}^{A} \Big[ w_{pos,a} \cdot y_{i,a}^{binary} \cdot \log(\sigma(\hat{y}_{i,a})) \\
&\quad + (1 - y_{i,a}^{binary}) \cdot \log(1 - \sigma(\hat{y}_{i,a})) \Big],
\end{split}
\end{equation}

where $y_{i,a}^{binary} = \mathbb{1}[y_{i,a} > \tau]$ is the binary ground truth derived by thresholding power at $\tau=15$W, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, and $w_{pos,a}$ is the per-appliance positive class weight computed as the ratio of negative to positive samples: $w_{pos,a} = (1 - r_a) / r_a$, where $r_a$ is the ON-time ratio for appliance $a$. This adaptive weighting prevents the trivial ``always-OFF'' solution for appliances with low duty cycles.

The deployed checkpoints were trained with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine annealing with warm restarts ($T_0=10$, $T_{mult}=2$, $\eta_{min}=10^{-6}$), gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, batch size 4096, up to 100 epochs and early stopping with a patience of 25 epochs on the validation loss. Inputs are windows of 512 aggregate-power samples at 8-second resolution (Section~\ref{subsubsec:datasets}).

\paragraph{Training Efficiency} Pre-converting the arrays to tensors, enabling cuDNN auto-tuning and reducing per-batch logging cut the epoch time on the 380K-window REFIT training set from 16 to 2.5 minutes on an NVIDIA A100 (6.4$\times$ speed-up, Figure~\ref{fig:training_speedup}), which made the search over loss configurations practical.


\paragraph{Controlled experiments and reproducibility} The ablation, baseline, robustness and adaptation experiments retrain every model under one protocol: Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine annealing to 1\% of the initial rate, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, mixed-precision (FP16) training, batch size 512, at most 100 epochs for SustDataED2 and UK-DALE and 25 epochs for REFIT (reduced protocol: every third training window), early stopping with a patience of 25 epochs on the validation loss, and checkpoint selection by validation loss only; the test split is evaluated once. Seeds 2026--2030 are used for SustDataED2 and UK-DALE (three seeds for the EDTCN on REFIT, two for the REFIT baselines). Training ran with PyTorch 2.6 (CUDA 12.4) on an NVIDIA RTX 3050 6~GB laptop GPU (150 training runs, about 31 hours in total). Confidence intervals use a block bootstrap over 32-window blocks (1000 resamples), and differences between models are tested with a paired block bootstrap on identical test windows.

\paragraph{Preprocessing} The same pipeline is applied to all datasets. (i) \textit{Resampling:} channels are averaged onto a regular 8-second grid and gaps shorter than one minute (up to 7 samples) are forward-filled. (ii) \textit{Missing values:} remaining gaps are set to 0~W in SustDataED2; in UK-DALE, windows with more than 10\% missing samples are discarded and the rest zero-filled. (iii) \textit{Synchronization:} SustDataED2 timestamps are rounded to whole seconds before resampling (Section~\ref{subsubsec:SustDataED2_preprocessing}). (iv) \textit{Windowing:} 512-step windows, non-overlapping for SustDataED2 and UK-DALE and with a 256-step stride for REFIT; splits are by household (REFIT) or chronological, so overlapping windows never straddle two splits. SustDataED2 windows in which more than half of the aggregate samples are below 1~W are removed. (v) \textit{Normalization:} the aggregate is standardized with training-split statistics; targets stay in Watts. (vi) \textit{Thresholds:} no activation filtering is applied; the 15~W ON/OFF threshold is used only for state metrics, and the optional post-processing threshold is selected on the validation split (Section~\ref{subsubsec:threshold_optimization}). (vii) \textit{Voltage:} only active power is used, so mains-voltage differences do not enter the model. The SustDataED2 aggregate is the sum of its twelve metered channels (no unmetered load), whereas REFIT and UK-DALE use measured mains with unmetered consumption, which is one reason for the lower SustDataED2 error.

\paragraph{SustDataED2 (primary)} The SustDataED2 residential dataset~\cite{Pereira2023} contains 12 appliances with raw sampling intervals of 1--6~s. After resampling to 8~s and windowing, the chronological 70/15/15\% split yields 1,416, 303 and 304 windows. The timestamp irregularities handled in Section~\ref{subsubsec:SustDataED2_preprocessing} are part of the preprocessing.

\subsubsection{Ablation Study}
\label{subsubsec:ablation}

To isolate the contribution of each design choice, ten variants were trained with identical data, seeds, optimizer, epochs and validation-based checkpoint selection (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Differences are tested with a paired block bootstrap on identical test windows, averaged over the five seeds.

\textit{Architecture.} Dilation is the decisive ingredient: adding exponentially dilated convolutions to a plain 6-block TCN (EDTCN with the same MSE+MAE loss) lowers the MAE from 5.58 to 4.99~W on SustDataED2 (11\%) and from 16.22 to 14.29~W on UK-DALE (12\%), and raises the F1 from 0.746 to 0.830 and from 0.534 to 0.560. Residual connections need dilation to be useful: without it they do not improve the plain TCN (6.61 and 17.54~W) and make training less stable (F1 standard deviation 0.18 across seeds on SustDataED2). Extending the receptive field from 67 to 259 steps (4 to 6 blocks) has no measurable effect on SustDataED2 (4.92 versus 4.99~W) but reduces the UK-DALE error from 15.48 to 14.29~W, in line with its long washing-machine and dishwasher cycles.

\textit{Loss function.} With the 6-block EDTCN fixed, MAE as the training loss gives the lowest MAE (4.68~W and 13.51~W) but underestimates the energy (SAE 0.46 and 0.54, about 2.5 times the 0.18--0.25 of the other losses), with high precision and low recall; the standard focal loss ($\gamma=2$) is the weakest objective. The remaining losses (MSE+MAE, MSE, type-weighted MSE and the proposed loss) lie within 0.4~W (SustDataED2) and 0.8~W (UK-DALE) of each other. Within the focal family, the device-type weights help on UK-DALE (15.01 versus 15.25~W) and are neutral on SustDataED2 (focal loss without type weights: $-0.015$~W, interval $[-0.032,-0.001]$~W, far below the seed spread of 0.33~W; the asterisk in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} marks statistical, not practical, significance). Compared with plain MSE+MAE the proposed loss has a slightly higher MAE but raises recall in all but one of the eight dataset--type groups (Table~\ref{tab:loss_by_type}), most clearly for regular-use appliances on REFIT (recall 0.52 to 0.71, F1 0.28 to 0.36) and burst appliances on SustDataED2 (0.38 to 0.46), while F1 stays within 0.01 elsewhere. It is thus a recall-oriented objective for event-driven services, whereas the accuracy gain over prior architectures comes from the dilated residual architecture itself.

\subsubsection{Comparison with Baselines under an Identical Protocol}
\label{subsubsec:sota_comparison}

Overall MAE values from the literature depend on the appliance set, window length, sampling interval and split, so numbers copied from different papers are not comparable and are not tabulated. Instead, Seq2Point~\cite{Kelly2018}, UNet-NILM~\cite{Bonfigli2019} and BERT4NILM~\cite{Yue2020} were re-implemented as compact networks with the same interface (512-step input, one output sequence per appliance) and trained exactly like the EDTCN: same windows, splits, optimizer, epoch budget, seeds, checkpoint selection and MSE+MAE loss. They are our own untuned re-implementations, not the original code or weights. Table~\ref{tab:baselines} and Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline_comparison} show that on every dataset the EDTCN has a lower MAE and a higher F1 than every baseline; with the deployed loss the MAE is 26\% (SustDataED2), 9\% (REFIT) and 11\% (UK-DALE) lower than the strongest baseline (BERT4NILM-style in all cases), and with the matched MSE+MAE loss 31\%, 12\% and 16\%. All differences are significant in the paired test ($p<0.001$) and many times larger than the seed standard deviation.

The EDTCN is also the most computationally demanding of the compared networks (397~M MACs versus 52~M, 75~M and 85~M for the baselines, Table~\ref{tab:baselines}), which is the price of its long receptive field. The ablation shows how this cost can be traded: the 4-block variant needs 282~M MACs (29\% fewer) and 545,804 parameters (12 output heads), and with the MSE+MAE loss it still reaches 4.92~W on SustDataED2 and 15.48~W on UK-DALE, below the strongest baseline on both datasets (7.23 and 16.95~W). The on-device latency of the deployed model is reported in Section~\ref{subsubsec:deployment_details}.

\subsubsection{The False Positive Problem in Regression-Based NILM}
\label{subsubsec:false_positive_problem}

A critical limitation discovered during evaluation is that regression models exhibit a systematic bias toward predicting small positive power values when appliances are OFF, the ``slightly-ON-everywhere'' phenomenon. It stems from the squared-error component of the training objective. A squared-error loss is minimized by the conditional mean, and for a rarely used appliance that is uncertain to be ON the conditional mean is small but positive: predicting 10~W for an OFF appliance (true power 0~W) costs little, whereas confidently predicting 0~W when the appliance unexpectedly turns ON (true power 500~W) is penalized quadratically. The model therefore hedges and emits small positive values everywhere. A pure absolute-error loss does not behave this way, because it converges to the conditional median, which is near zero for such appliances (Section~\ref{subsec:interpretation}); the MAE-trained variant of the ablation accordingly has the highest precision and the lowest recall, but it underestimates the energy.

This behavior is quantified on the unseen REFIT test houses: at the 15~W state threshold the unprocessed regression output of the deployed model has a pooled precision of 30.6\% (all nine appliances and all 59,927 test windows) together with a pooled recall of 83.8\% and a MAE of 15.66~W, i.e. most activations are found but 69.4\% of the ON decisions are false alarms. The per-appliance breakdown shows where they come from: the microwave reaches only 5.8\% precision, the computer 6.7\%, the washing machine 12.8\% and the tumble dryer 13.8\%, whereas the fridge (54.0\%) and the kettle (19.4\%) are much cleaner. The reason is the ``ghost'' output of 5--15~W that the model emits during OFF periods of rarely used appliances: it is too small to affect the MAE appreciably but sufficient to cross the 15~W threshold when it persists over many time steps.

This observation has a methodological consequence that applies to NILM evaluation in general: MAE alone cannot reveal this failure mode, because a model can have a good MAE and still produce a state signal that is not directly usable. For this reason all experiments in this paper report MAE together with RMSE, SAE, NDE, precision, recall and F1 for every appliance of every dataset, including the appliances that are not detected (for example the SustDataED2 vacuum cleaner and the UK-DALE laptop and router, F1 = 0). We recommend that multi-metric, per-appliance reporting become the norm in NILM comparisons.

\subsubsection{Post-Processing Optimization}
\label{subsubsec:threshold_optimization}

A simple post-processing threshold $\tau$ (power estimates below $\tau$ are set to zero) trades recall for precision without retraining and without additional memory on the meter. To avoid tuning on test data, $\tau$ is selected on the validation split of each dataset and then applied to the test split (Table~\ref{tab:threshold_sweep}). The validation-selected thresholds are 10~W for REFIT, 20~W for SustDataED2 and 25~W for UK-DALE, giving test F1 values of 0.409, 0.845 and 0.519 (without post-processing, i.e.\ at $\tau=15$~W: 0.448, 0.824 and 0.531).


The F1 curve has a broad plateau: for $\tau$ between 10 and 25~W the test F1 varies by only 0.06~on REFIT, 0.07 on SustDataED2 and 0.03 on UK-DALE, so the system is not sensitive to the exact value and a threshold of 15--20~W is a robust default. Precision increases monotonically with $\tau$ (REFIT: 0.09 at 0~W, 0.31 at 15~W, 0.35 at 20~W and 0.38 at 25~W) while recall decreases (1.00, 0.84, 0.69 and 0.55), so the application can select the operating point: a low threshold for event-sensitive services, a higher one for reports in which false alarms are costly. Because the threshold is a scalar applied to the output, it has negligible cost on the F1C200s.

\subsubsection{Robustness to Unseen Households and Datasets}
\label{subsubsec:robustness}

This section examines how well a trained model works in an unseen household, how much state-detection ability transfers to another dataset without retraining, and how little target data is needed to adapt it.

\paragraph{Unseen households} The REFIT test houses 19--21 and the validation house 18 are never used for training, so every REFIT result is already an unseen-household result. Table~\ref{tab:unseen_houses} breaks it down per house (appliances present only): the MAE ranges from about 14 to 24~W and the F1 from 0.47 to 0.68, and the deployed checkpoint and the retrained model agree to within 5\% in MAE, so the variation comes from the households (appliance mix, unmetered load) and not from a particular training run.


\paragraph{Leave-one-house-out} To measure the cost of a new household, five training houses (2, 5, 9, 13 and 16) were held out one at a time and a fresh model was trained on the others (Table~\ref{tab:loho}). Compared with the reference model that saw the house, the held-out model has a +1.0~W higher MAE on average (range +0.6 to +1.6~W) and an F1 that differs by -0.02 (-0.04 to +0.02). Because the held-out models were trained for 8 epochs and the reference for 25, this gap is an upper bound; the 16 training households are diverse enough that an additional household costs little accuracy. SustDataED2 and UK-DALE are single households, so a within-dataset unseen-household test is not possible there.


\paragraph{Cross-dataset transfer and adaptation protocol} A source EDTCN with the proposed loss was trained (two seeds) on the five appliances shared by REFIT and UK-DALE (fridge, washing machine, dishwasher, microwave, kettle), once with each dataset as the source; the targets are UK-DALE house 1 and REFIT test houses 19--21. On a held-out block of target data we evaluate \textit{zero-shot} use of the source model, \textit{adaptation} with the first $k=1,3,7$ days of the target household (21, 63 and 148 windows), and training from scratch on the same days. The next 300 windows serve for validation and the later windows form the evaluation block, identical for every $k$; no window is shared between adaptation, validation and evaluation in any of the 24 configurations. The adaptation methods are \textit{heads only} (trunk frozen, learning rate $3\cdot10^{-4}$), \textit{full fine-tuning} (learning rate $10^{-4}$) and fine-tuning with elastic weight consolidation (EWC), which adds the penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_i(\theta_i-\theta_i^{*})^2$ with the diagonal Fisher information $F_i$ of the source model (640 source windows, unit mean) and source weights $\theta^{*}$; $\lambda\in\{10^2,\dots,10^7\}$ is the largest value whose validation loss is within 5\% of the best. All runs use 40 epochs, batches of at most 64 windows and the lowest-validation-loss checkpoint. Adaptation is computed offline; the meter only receives the exported INT8 model.

\paragraph{Results} Table~\ref{tab:transfer} summarizes the experiments. Zero-shot, the source models detect the target appliances with an F1 of 0.48--0.57, 86--88\% of the F1 of the in-domain models (0.55--0.66). The MAE is less informative because the appliances are OFF most of the time (predicting zero gives 12--24~W, similar to zero-shot). With seven days of data the best adapted model improves the zero-shot MAE by 1.6~W on UK-DALE and by 0.2, 2.2 and 6.0~W on REFIT houses 19, 20 and 21, with the F1 at 0.50--0.63. Training from scratch on the same days is clearly worse (F1 0.05--0.53, MAE up to 17~W higher), so the source model carries most of the useful knowledge; heads-only adaptation is the safest option for one day of data, whereas full fine-tuning or EWC pays off at seven days.

Full fine-tuning increases the MAE on the source dataset by 3--10~W (catastrophic forgetting). With one or three days of target data, EWC reduces this forgetting by up to 57\% at equal or better target accuracy (REFIT house 20, one day: forgetting 5.3 $\rightarrow$ 2.3~W, MAE 15.7 $\rightarrow$ 15.3~W; house 21: MAE 29.4 $\rightarrow$ 27.7~W with one day, 32.0 $\rightarrow$ 28.2~W with three days); with seven days the validation rule selects a weaker penalty and the reduction is at most 12\%. EWC thus limits but does not remove forgetting, and freezing the trunk limits the change of the source MAE to at most 2.7~W. Adaptation does not help every appliance: the UK-DALE dishwasher runs only about 2\% of the time and its zero-shot MAE of 18.1~W becomes 18.5--24.0~W after adaptation.


\paragraph{Behavior over time} Dividing the chronological test split into four consecutive quarters (UK-DALE test period about four months), the MAE of the retrained models is 5.5, 5.5, 4.9 and 5.2~W on SustDataED2 and 14.8, 14.9, 16.7 and 13.6~W on UK-DALE, without a monotonic degradation. Multi-year drift and changing appliance inventories cannot be assessed with the available data; adaptation is the intended mechanism and long-term field validation is future work.

\subsubsection{SustDataED2 Dataset Preprocessing Challenge}
\label{subsubsec:SustDataED2_preprocessing}

The twelve SustDataED2 appliance files are logged by independent sensors whose clocks differ by microseconds, which makes timestamp-based joins fail. Rounding every timestamp to the nearest second before the 8-second resampling (\texttt{dt.round('1s')}) gives all twelve appliances identical indices, while the original sampling intervals of 1--6~s remain finer than the 8~s grid.

\subsubsection{Embedded Deployment Pipeline}
\label{subsubsec:deployment_details}

Deploying the models on the F1C200s (ARM926EJ-S, 64~MB RAM, no FPU) required four stages.

\paragraph{Stage 1: Graph Optimization via PNNX}
The PyTorch graph was converted to the PNNX representation~\cite{pnnx}, which fuses consecutive operations (e.g., Conv + BatchNorm + ReLU) and thereby reduces the activation writes to DRAM that dominate cost on CPU-only systems; the result was converted to the lightweight NCNN format~\cite{ncnn2024}.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Calibration for Quantization Range Determination}
A calibration set of about 500 windows was drawn from the validation split (half selected by appliance activity, half evenly spaced) so that ON and OFF states of all appliances are represented. Activation ranges were chosen by minimizing the Kullback--Leibler divergence between the FP32 and the clipped, quantized activation histograms~\cite{migacz2017tensorrt}, with the analytical ACIQ clipping criterion as an alternative. The NCNN quantization tools silently skip 1-D convolutions, so these were expressed as exactly equivalent $(1\times k)$ 2-D convolutions with the same weights, after which every convolution can be quantized.

\paragraph{Stage 3: INT8 Post-Training Quantization}
Weights were quantized per output channel and activations per tensor with the symmetric mapping
\begin{equation}
x_{int8} = \text{clip}(\text{round}(x_{fp32} / s), -128, 127)
\end{equation}
using the scale factors $s$ from the calibration table. The INT8 weights occupy 0.72--0.98~MB, 3.9$\times$ less than FP32 (sizes in Table~\ref{tab:int8_pc}), which evaluates the true INT8 models on the complete test splits on a PC. With ACIQ clipping the regression MAE changes by -0.8\%, +2.5\% and -0.0\% on REFIT, SustDataED2 and UK-DALE and the F1 stays within 0.01 of FP32; KL clipping is equally good on REFIT (+0.7\%) but raises the SustDataED2 MAE by 33\%. Keeping the input projection (and, where the validation split favors it, the output convolutions) in FP32, a choice made on the validation split only, keeps every regression model at or below +0.4\% of the FP32 MAE (REFIT: 2.0\% below) with weights of at most 0.90~MB. The classification networks are more sensitive: with all convolutions in INT8 the macro-F1 changes by -0.128 and -0.182 (KL clipping, SustDataED2 and UK-DALE), whereas the mixed-precision models are within 0.035 of FP32. On the PC one window takes 6.5--8.3~ms in INT8 and 6.6--9.0~ms in FP32 (single thread), so INT8 brings no speed-up there and none is claimed; its effect on the F1C200s, which has no SIMD unit, was not isolated. The execution time, memory and power on the F1C200s are reported below.

\paragraph{Effect of operator fusion}
Fusion (Conv, BatchNorm and ReLU merged; 98 to 66 layers for the 12-appliance models) is lossless: FP32 and INT8 MAE and F1 are identical to the third decimal in the three NCNN forms, no PC latency benefit could be shown, and its effect on the F1C200s was not measured (details and Table~\ref{tab:fusion} in the Supplementary Material). The accuracy changes in this section are therefore caused by quantization alone.


\paragraph{Stage 4: Cross-Compilation for ARM Architecture}
The quantized NCNN model was cross-compiled for the ARM926EJ-S with arm-linux-gnueabi-g++ (\texttt{-mcpu=arm926ej-s -mfloat-abi=soft}) and statically linked. As the F1C200s has no FPU, the convolutions run in INT8 integer arithmetic and the few remaining floating-point operations (input scaling, quantization scales, de-quantization, threshold) use soft-float routines, whose cost is negligible against $3.8\times10^{8}$ multiply--accumulate operations. The deployment comprises the executable (28~KB), the model structure file (5~KB) and the quantized weights (850~KB), 883~KB in total. A soft-float build executed on a PC with an ARM926EJ-S CPU model reproduces the outputs of the PC build (mean absolute difference below $5\times10^{-4}$, ON/OFF agreement of at least 99.996\%; Table~\ref{tab:arm_build} in the Supplementary Material); no timing, power or temperature is derived from these runs.

\subsubsection{Summary and Key Findings}
\label{subsubsec:nilm_summary}

The EDTCN is 9--26\% more accurate in MAE than the strongest of three baselines trained under the identical protocol, and the ablation attributes the gain mainly to the exponentially dilated residual blocks with a 259-step receptive field; the device-type-weighted loss raises the recall of regular-use and burst appliances rather than lowering the MAE. Reporting MAE together with RMSE, SAE, NDE, precision, recall and F1 for every appliance exposes behavior that MAE hides, such as the 30.6\% pooled precision of the unthresholded REFIT output. A house unseen in training costs about 1~W of MAE, zero-shot transfer retains 86--88\% of the in-domain F1, and a few days of adaptation lower the MAE with limited forgetting under EWC. Very low-power devices ($<$2~W) and extremely rare appliances (ON time $<$0.5\%) remain hard to detect. The embedded deployment is described in Section~\ref{subsubsec:deployment_details}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpreting the NILM Results}
\label{subsec:interpretation}

\textit{Temporal context drives accuracy.} Enlarging the receptive field by exponential dilation from 31 to 259 steps lowers the MAE by 11--12\% on both datasets, and extending it from 67 to 259 steps helps on UK-DALE (1.2~W), whose washing machine and dishwasher run long programs, but not on SustDataED2, whose appliances are short bursts and steady loads. Residual connections alone brought no improvement, whereas with dilation they allow six blocks to train reliably (seed spread 0.1--0.2~W).

\textit{The loss selects an operating point.} An absolute-error loss converges to the conditional median, which is near zero for an appliance that is OFF most of the time, so MAE training gives the lowest MAE but underestimates energy (SAE 0.46--0.54 versus 0.19--0.25). Squared-error terms preserve energy, and the device-type weights and focal factor raise the recall of regular-use and burst appliances (REFIT regular-use from 0.52 to 0.71) at a MAE cost of 0.4--0.7~W; the preferable loss depends on the service (energy accuracy for billing, recall for event-driven functions).

\textit{Datasets differ in the signal.} SustDataED2 has no unmetered load and distinct appliance levels, whereas REFIT and UK-DALE contain unmetered consumption and overlapping signatures (500--2500~W), so errors should be compared within a dataset. State detection transfers better across datasets than amplitude, which depends on the appliance models and background load and can be corrected by a few days of adaptation (Section~\ref{subsubsec:robustness}).

\subsection{Deployment Considerations}
\label{subsec:scalability}

\textit{Efficiency and bandwidth.} The third generation has a 35.9\% lower idle current (86.00 versus 134.24~mA) and a 71.6\% lower active current (102.68 versus 361.48~mA) than the discrete single-board design, which matters because the aggregate consumption of widespread smart meters is itself a load on the grid. Transmitting only classification results (about 1~KB per event) instead of raw waveforms (about 44~kHz in the prototype capture) reduces the data volume by several orders of magnitude \cite{Kelly2024}.

\textit{Updates and scale.} The meter runs embedded Linux and is updated by overwriting the deployed system; firmware signature verification, device-level model-version reporting and controlled rollout are future work. Per million meters, the 15-min values of the forecasting service amount to about 0.8~GB per day and a full model update of 0.72--0.98~MB per meter to roughly 1~TB of traffic. These are estimates: backend capacity at fleet scale has not been validated by load tests, and no capacity claim is made.

\textit{Privacy and failure handling.} Raw high-frequency waveforms are processed on the meter and not uploaded; the head-end system receives appliance classifications, estimated power or energy, event timestamps and aggregated consumption. This reduces the exposure of raw measurements but does not prevent inference of occupancy and routines from appliance-level events, so NGSM is a privacy-risk-reduction architecture, not a guarantee of anonymity (threat analysis in the Supplementary Material). After a power outage the journaling file system restores integrity, corrupted binaries or model weights fail to load rather than execute, and during network interruptions the meter buffers its results locally and checks for duplicates after reconnection (Table~\ref{tab:failures}); a hardware watchdog, automated rollback and a validated recovery framework are future work. Adaptation to a new household is computed off the meter and only the exported INT8 model is installed; distributing updated models to many meters is outside the scope of this study.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

\textit{NILM evaluation.} The SustDataED2 aggregate is the sum of the metered channels (no unmetered load); SustDataED2 and UK-DALE are single households, so unseen-household behavior is measured only on REFIT; the REFIT controlled experiments use a reduced training protocol; the baselines are our own untuned re-implementations; the cross-dataset and adaptation study covers five shared appliances and two source--target pairs; and multi-year drift and changes in the appliance inventory could not be assessed. The scope is single-phase residential loads on 230~V, 50~Hz installations; three-phase systems, industrial loads (for which the models and the 15~W threshold would have to be re-derived) and 60~Hz grids were not evaluated. The one-head-per-appliance design adds about 12{,}400 parameters and increases the inference time by 8--20\% from 9 to 12 appliances, which together with the 64~MB memory (66.77\% used) bounds the number of appliances per meter.

\textit{Hardware and scope.} The hardware is an uncertified proof-of-concept prototype, and this work addresses the feasibility of NILM inference on a low-cost, low-power SoC, not a complete metrological and environmental characterization of the meter. Power was read with a multimeter at board level. Thermal behavior, multi-day stability (the 24-h soak test is a single run), the timing of the real-time tasks while an inference runs (the single core is saturated for about two minutes in every fifteen), a per-subsystem power breakdown, an equal-workload comparison with other platforms, surge and certification tests, and a comparison of the harmonic indices with a calibrated power-quality analyzer require instrumentation that was not available for this study; they are not claimed here and are planned for the pilot deployments. The batch implementation delivers results about two minutes after each interval and does not target sub-minute event detection, and the forecasting module depends on network connectivity. The security measures (JWT authentication, TLS, tamper detection) are not a complete assurance framework (details in the Supplementary Material).

\textit{Future work} includes hardware-aware architecture search, pruning and distillation to reduce CPU load, an incremental activation-caching implementation, multi-meter federated learning for feeder-level analytics, on-device forecasting, systematic security evaluation, and pilot field deployments to gather data on long-term reliability, model drift and user acceptance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work presented NGSM, a hardware--software smart-metering framework that runs NILM entirely on a low-cost, low-power Allwinner F1C200s System-in-Package. The custom design reduces the active power to 0.513~W (102.68~mA at 5~V), 85.3\% below a Raspberry Pi 4 and 71.6\% below a Raspberry Pi Zero 2W baseline. The EDTCN (707K parameters) reaches $5.34\pm0.10$~W, $15.38\pm0.21$~W and $15.01\pm0.15$~W MAE (F1 0.81, 0.46 and 0.55) on SustDataED2, REFIT and UK-DALE, 9--26\% below the strongest of three baselines trained under the identical protocol; the ablation attributes the gain to the dilated residual architecture, while the device-type-weighted focal loss raises the recall of regular-use and burst appliances. A held-out household costs about 1~W of MAE, zero-shot transfer retains 86--88\% of the in-domain F1, and a few days of adaptation with elastic weight consolidation reduce the error with limited forgetting. Calibrated INT8 quantization shrinks the weights 3.9 times to below 1~MB with regression MAE within 3\% of FP32 on the complete test splits, which enables deployment on 64~MB RAM without GPU or FPU. Because MAE alone can be misleading in NILM, the study reports MAE, RMSE, SAE, NDE, precision, recall and F1 for all appliances and datasets, including failure cases. NGSM also provides IEC 61000-4-7 harmonic analysis and a cloud forecasting module. Overall, appliance-level analytics with near-real-time delivery is feasible under strict embedded constraints; field validation, certification and the measurements listed in the limitations remain future work.
