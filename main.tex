\begin{abstract}
Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (NILM) enables appliance-level energy disaggregation from aggregate smart-meter data without per-device sensors, supporting demand response and energy-efficiency services. Existing deep-learning NILM systems, however, rely on GPU-accelerated or high-power embedded platforms (4--10~W), and cloud inference exposes high-resolution consumption data. This paper presents NGSM, an edge smart-metering system that executes NILM entirely on a low-cost embedded SoC without GPU or FPU support. NGSM deploys an Enhanced Dilated Temporal Convolutional Network (EDTCN) on an ARM926EJ-S System-in-Package (Allwinner F1C200s, 64~MB RAM). The network stacks six exponentially dilated residual blocks (259-step receptive field, 34.5~minutes at 8~s sampling) and is trained with a device-type-weighted focal loss. A controlled ablation shows that the dilated residual architecture provides the main accuracy gain, whereas the loss moves the operating point toward higher recall on burst and regular-use appliances. Post-training INT8 quantization with calibrated clipping and compiler-level fusion reduces the weight storage by a factor of 3.9 to below 1~MB with essentially unchanged regression accuracy (complete test splits). NGSM additionally performs on-device FFT-based harmonic analysis and offloads long-term load forecasting to the cloud. Under a documented chronological and household-level protocol on SustDataED2, REFIT and UK-DALE, the EDTCN reaches $5.34\pm0.10$~W, $15.38\pm0.21$~W and $15.01\pm0.15$~W MAE (F1 of 0.81, 0.46 and 0.55; mean $\pm$ std over seeds), which is 9--26\% below the strongest of three baselines (Seq2Point-, UNet-NILM- and BERT4NILM-style networks) re-implemented and trained under the identical protocol. Leave-one-house-out tests, zero-shot cross-dataset transfer and few-day adaptation with elastic weight consolidation quantify robustness to unseen households. The meter draws 0.513~W during active inference at a \$3--5 chip cost (volume pricing), a practical path toward scalable edge-deployed NILM.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Smart grids are evolving from automated billing toward Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) 2.0 networks~\cite{ami2.0} with distributed intelligence at the grid edge. In this context, real-time refers to the continuous acquisition, processing and exchange of measurement and control data with latencies from milliseconds for local edge processing to a few seconds for system coordination, so that load monitoring, fault detection and demand response can follow changing grid conditions instead of relying on delayed batch processing. In NGSM the metrology, harmonic analysis and display operate at this millisecond-to-second scale, whereas the per-appliance NILM service is a near-real-time analytics layer that delivers its results within about two minutes after each 15-minute reporting interval (Section~\ref{subsubsec:latency}). The growing number of distributed energy resources increases the demand for granular monitoring at the grid edge~\cite{Market2025}, and the edge-AI market for smart grids is projected to grow by more than 24\% per year through 2034~\cite{Precedence2025}, driven by the need to process IoT energy data close to its source without overloading networks and central servers~\cite{JISEM2025}. Many edge-oriented smart-grid studies, however, emphasize architectural decentralization without testing whether modern deep-learning models can run under realistic metering power and memory limits.

Many commercial deployments still rely on cloud-centric analytics: for high-resolution monitoring and NILM, voltage and current measurements are sent to remote servers, which adds latency and bandwidth demand, concentrates failure risk and creates an attack surface; centralized smart-grid databases are increasingly targeted by ransomware and false-data-injection attacks~\cite{EnergiesSec2025}. Fine-grained load profiles also reveal household activities, appliance usage and occupancy~\cite{MDPI2025}. Moving AI to the meter addresses these concerns but is difficult on standard microcontroller-based meters with limited processing, memory and energy budgets, and edge platforms often meet latency and memory bottlenecks on continuous high-frequency data~\cite{EdgeNILM2025}.

Lightweight networks for edge NILM generalize poorly to unseen households (cold-start and domain shift~\cite{IEEE2024}), and centralized training requires collecting user data, which motivates collaborative, privacy-preserving learning such as federated learning~\cite{SciRep2025}. A gap therefore remains for holistic solutions that jointly address the computational constraints of edge devices, adaptability across households and the privacy requirements of modern smart-grid regulation.


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Smart Metering Architectures}

The transition from traditional electromechanical meters to Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) has been driven by the need for bidirectional communication and automated billing. Early smart meters focused primarily on remote reading capabilities to reduce operational costs. However, the advent of AMI 2.0 has introduced a requirement for real-time monitoring and distributed intelligence \cite{ami2.0}. Modern architectures are now evolving beyond simple data logging to support complex grid-edge applications such as demand response and dynamic load management, requiring significantly enhanced computational capabilities comparable to industrial IoT gateways.

To address the latency and bandwidth limitations of cloud-centric models, research has shifted toward edge-centric architectures. Xu et al. proposed a distributed framework where intelligent electronic devices (IEDs) perform localized state estimation, effectively reducing the dependency on central control centers \cite{Xu2024}. Similarly, recent hardware implementations have focused on embedding high-frequency analytics directly into the meter. Schirmer et al. developed an FPGA-based smart meter capable of real-time disaggregation, demonstrating high precision but highlighting the complexity of FPGA integration \cite{Schirmer2025}. In parallel, Mallala et al. presented an IoT-based power quality analyzer for local signal processing, addressing the need for cost-effective monitoring solutions \cite{Mallala2025}. These works collectively illustrate the trend toward shifting computational workloads to the grid edge.

\subsection{Data Analytics and AI in Smart Grids}

\subsubsection{Harmonics and Power Quality Analysis}
The detection of power quality disturbances has traditionally relied on specialized, high-cost analyzers. However, recent initiatives have sought to integrate these capabilities into standard smart meters. Moustafa et al. demonstrated a web-based monitoring system utilizing a Raspberry Pi to classify harmonic distortions and voltage anomalies \cite{Moustafa2025PQ}. While effective, such implementations often employ single-board computers with relatively high power consumption, creating a need for solutions that provide equivalent spectral analysis on power-optimized industrial platforms.

\subsubsection{Energy Demand Forecasting}
Short-term load forecasting (STLF) is critical for grid stability. Deep learning models have largely replaced traditional statistical methods to capture non-linear temporal dependencies. Hybrid architectures, such as those combining Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks with Transformers, have been proposed to leverage both local feature extraction and global attention mechanisms \cite{HybridLSTMTrans2025}. Similarly, ensemble methods integrating LSTM with XGBoost have been explored to enhance robustness against demand volatility \cite{HybridLSTMXGB2025}. Despite their accuracy, these complex models often incur significant computational overheads, necessitating a careful balance between predictive performance and resource utilization in edge deployment scenarios.

\subsubsection{Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (Deep Learning)}
In the domain of Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (NILM), deep neural networks have surpassed combinatorial optimization methods. Convolutional architectures like Sequence-to-Point (Seq2Point) have proven effective in mapping aggregate power windows to appliance activations \cite{Zhang2018}. More recently, Transformer-based models such as BERT4NILM \cite{Yue2020}, NILMFormer \cite{Sykiotis2024}, and ELECTRIcity \cite{Massidda2022} have been adapted to model long-term temporal dependencies in energy data. While achieving state-of-the-art accuracy, these models typically fail to meet the strict memory and latency constraints of embedded smart meters, often requiring GPU acceleration for efficient inference.

\subsubsection{Edge Deployment of NILM Models}
The practical deployment of deep learning-based NILM on resource-constrained hardware remains an underexplored challenge. Recent benchmarking studies have evaluated NILM algorithms on standard edge platforms such as the Raspberry Pi 4, demonstrating feasible inference times but with power consumption in the range of 4 to 7 Watts \cite{EdgeNILM2025}. To achieve sub-second inference latency, other approaches rely on dedicated AI accelerators such as the NVIDIA Jetson Nano, which provides GPU-accelerated inference for complex architectures but at a cost exceeding \$99 and power consumption of 5 to 10 Watts \cite{Xu2024VMD}. These power envelopes and unit costs render such platforms unsuitable for mass-scale utility deployment, where millions of meters must operate continuously within stringent energy budgets. The gap between laboratory-grade prototyping hardware and industrially viable, sub-Watt (< 1W) embedded solutions capable of executing deep learning inference remains a critical barrier to the widespread adoption of intelligent energy disaggregation.

\subsubsection{Privacy and Adaptation in NILM}
The centralization of high-resolution energy data raises significant privacy concerns, as fine-grained load profiles can reveal sensitive household activities \cite{MDPI2025}. Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a solution to enable collaborative training without sharing raw data \cite{Li2024, SciRep2025}. However, privacy alone does not ensure long-term reliability; models must also adapt to changing household environments. Techniques such as Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) have been proposed to support continual learning \cite{Kirkpatrick2017}, yet few studies have successfully integrated these adaptive mechanisms into a cohesive, resource-constrained metering system covering the entire pipeline from signal acquisition to inference.

\subsection{Contribution and Positioning}
\label{subsec:contribution}

Despite the advancements in edge computing, a gap remains in unifying hardware efficiency, advanced AI, and robustness across households and datasets. Many existing solutions either rely on power-hungry prototyping hardware (e.g., Raspberry Pi 4) which exceeds the energy budget of mass-deployed meters, or utilize low-power microcontrollers that lack the capacity for complex NILM inference \cite{EdgeNILM2025}. Furthermore, achieving state-of-the-art disaggregation accuracy on resource-constrained industrial-grade embedded silicon without GPU or FPU remains a significant challenge. This work addresses this gap by demonstrating, for the first time, the successful deployment of a deep temporal convolutional network achieving high NILM accuracy on an ARM926EJ-S-based System-in-Package without FPU, GPU, or dedicated accelerator, at \$3-5 component cost (volume pricing) and 0.513W power consumption during active inference.

This work introduces the Next-Generation Smart Grid Meter (NGSM), a comprehensive framework that bridges this gap. Unlike previous prototypes, the NGSM is built on a custom industrial SiP architecture (Allwinner F1C200s), achieving significant reductions in active power consumption. It uniquely integrates an Enhanced Dilated Temporal Convolutional Network (EDTCN) for NILM with device-type-weighted focal loss, achieving lower error than baselines re-implemented under an identical protocol on three public datasets (SustDataED2, REFIT, UK-DALE), together with post-training INT8 quantization for embedded execution. The contributions of this work are validated through extensive experimental benchmarking across hardware, privacy, and analytics dimensions. First, regarding hardware efficiency, the transition to the custom SiP architecture reduces the active power consumption to 102.68~mA at 5~V (0.513~W), which is 71.6\% below the second-generation Raspberry Pi Zero 2W prototype and 85.3\% below the first-generation Raspberry Pi 4 prototype (Table~\ref{tab:hardwarebenchmarks}), supporting its suitability for mass deployment. Second, regarding algorithmic performance, the EDTCN-NILM achieves $5.34\pm0.10$~W MAE (F1 0.814) on SustDataED2, $15.38\pm0.21$~W (F1 0.464) on REFIT and $15.01\pm0.15$~W (F1 0.549) on UK-DALE (mean $\pm$ std over seeds), 9--26\% below the strongest of three baselines (Seq2Point-, UNet-NILM- and BERT4NILM-style networks) that were re-implemented and trained under the identical protocol. A controlled ablation attributes the gain mainly to the dilated residual architecture (259-step, 34.5-minute receptive field at 8~s sampling), while the device-type-weighted focal loss shifts the operating point toward higher recall on regular-use and burst appliances. Leave-one-house-out tests, zero-shot cross-dataset transfer and few-day adaptation quantify robustness to new households. The study also characterizes the false-positive behavior of regression-based NILM (30.6\% pooled precision on REFIT at a 15~W threshold) and reports every appliance, including those that are hard to detect, with SAE, NDE, precision, recall and F1 alongside MAE. Third, the system ensures grid stability through real-time harmonic analysis compliant with IEEE 519 standards (THD monitoring with FFT-based spectral decomposition). Fourth, the resource-aware forecasting module, utilizing a cloud-offloaded LSTM, achieves a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 12.70 W on UK-DALE. Fifth, embedded deployment on the F1C200s (ARM926EJ-S, 64MB RAM, no FPU) demonstrates a deep TCN executing NILM on an ARM926EJ-S-based SoC without FPU or dedicated accelerator, at a SiP cost of \$3-5 (volume pricing; the \$16 MangoPi development board used for prototyping is more expensive), with 101--123~s inference time per 68-minute window (33--40$\times$ faster than the data rate, 11--14\% CPU duty cycle at a 15-minute cadence), 66.77\% memory utilization, and 0.513W board-level power during inference via four-stage optimization (PNNX fusion, calibrated INT8 quantization that reduces the weight storage 3.9$\times$ to below 1~MB with regression MAE within 3\% of FP32, ARM cross-compilation). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the NGSM successfully delivers high-fidelity, privacy-aware, and transparently evaluated analytics within the strict operational constraints of modern smart grids.


\section{NGSM System Architecture}
\label{sec:system_arch}

The proposed Next-Generation Smart Grid Meter (NGSM) functions as a comprehensive Advanced Metering Infrastructure (AMI) 2.0 solution, designed to facilitate bidirectional communication and distributed processing. This architecture fundamentally shifts the computational load from centralized cloud servers to the edge of the grid, addressing the latency and scalability challenges inherent in traditional cloud-centric monitoring \cite{MDPI_NILM2025, ResearchGate2025}. The system topology is delineated into three primary domains: the End-User (Edge Node), the Communication Network (WAN), and the Head-End System (HES). As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:system_arch}, this distributed approach enables real-time responsiveness and reduces bandwidth congestion by processing high-frequency electrical data locally.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/img1.png}
    \caption{High-level architecture of the NGSM system illustrating the Edge-Cloud data flow.}
    \label{fig:system_arch}
\end{figure}

The fundamental unit of the architecture is the smart meter, which operates as an embedded Linux device integrating software and hardware within a System on Chip (SoC) architecture to support AI-integrated sustainable energy management \cite{ResearchGate2025}. The internal hardware architecture is composed of five critical functional blocks. First, the Analog Front End (AFE) is responsible for the high-frequency acquisition of voltage and current signals, performing Digital Signal Processing (DSP) and electricity tampering detection through live-neutral current comparison \cite{Energize2025}. Second, the Single Board Computer (SBC) hosts the primary SoC, storage, and communication modems, serving as the central processing unit capable of supporting complex AI models. These core elements are supported by a User Interaction module managing the TFT display, a Power Supply unit for regulation, and an Enclosure Tampering Detection system that utilizes electromechanical limit switches to trigger immediate interrupts upon unauthorized disassembly, a critical defense against physical attacks \cite{IJNSA2025}.

Data transmission between the edge nodes and the central infrastructure is mediated by a Wide Area Network (WAN) connecting to the Head-End System (HES). The HES acts as the centralized aggregation point, comprising a web-based dashboard and a smartphone mobile application. Beyond simple data logging, the HES executes high-level administrative tasks including time-of-use (ToU) billing calculation and secure remote payments. Furthermore, it handles advanced visualization by presenting hourly consumption data and appliance-level breakdowns to the consumer, a capability essential for modern utility customer engagement \cite{EY2025}. The HES also serves as the repository for Appliance Detection Analytics, receiving pre-processed classification results from the edge nodes. This strategy of transmitting only inference results rather than raw high-frequency waveforms significantly optimizes network bandwidth and reduces the processing latency typically associated with edge-cloud transfers \cite{MDPI_NILM2025}.


\section{Hardware Implementation}
\label{sec:hardware}

The development of the NGSM hardware followed an iterative engineering methodology, evolving through three distinct generations. This progression was driven by the necessity to balance computational performance for edge-AI tasks against the constraints of power consumption, thermal management, and mass-production viability.

\subsection{First Generation: Proof of Concept}
\label{subsec:gen1}

The initial development phase, designated as the first generation, focused on validating the embedded Linux software stack and the proprietary metrology algorithms without the immediate constraints of power efficiency or industrial form factors. The core processing unit for proof-of-concept prototyping was initially the Raspberry Pi 3 Model B \cite{pi3}, but the benchmarks reported in this study were evaluated on the Raspberry Pi 4 Model B \cite{pi4} to establish a high-performance baseline. This platform features a Quad-core Cortex-A72 (ARM v8) 64-bit SoC clocked at 1.8GHz and 8GB LPDDR4-3200 SDRAM. This high-performance specification provided the necessary computational overhead for compiling the initial C++ daemons and debugging the user-space applications directly on the target device. The utilization of such high-level processors is a documented methodology for rapid prototyping in smart grid applications, allowing developers to isolate algorithmic logic from hardware-level optimization constraints. As illustrated in the hardware block diagram in Figure \ref{fig:gen1_block}, the architecture utilizes the single board computer as the central coordinator for both signal acquisition and user interface management.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{figures/img3.png}
    \caption{First Generation NGSM Hardware Components illustrating the interface between the Raspberry Pi 3 and discrete analog sensors.}
    \label{fig:gen1_block}
\end{figure}

For the acquisition of electrical parameters, this generation relied on discrete sensing modules rather than an integrated analog front end, a strategy often employed to minimize prototyping costs in real-time energy loggers. Voltage sensing was achieved using the ZMPT101B active single-phase AC voltage sensor \cite{ZMPT101B}. This module utilizes a precision micro-voltage transformer with a 1000:1000 turns ratio and an operational amplifier circuit to step down the 220V grid voltage to a manageable analog signal suitable for digital conversion. Current measurements were performed using the SCT-013-000 split-core current transformer \cite{SCT013401}, a non-intrusive sensor capable of monitoring loads up to 100A with a linearity error of approximately 3 percent.

To verify sensor and peripheral integration during prototyping, real-time voltage and current streams were monitored through the embedded user interface and logging services as well as the working display unit. This verification step confirmed correct signal acquisition and end-to-end data flow prior to calibration and load testing.

With the display and sensor interfaces verified, the focus shifted to the accurate calibration of the load measurement. A critical design challenge with the SCT-013 family is the conversion of the induced secondary current into a voltage signal measurable by the analog-to-digital converter. The transformation ratio is governed by the relationship between the primary and secondary windings as defined in Equation \ref{eq:transformer_ratio}.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transformer_ratio}
\frac{I_{secondary}}{I_{primary}} = \frac{N_{primary}}{N_{secondary}}
\end{equation}

Since the SCT-013-100 model lacks an internal burden resistor, an external resistance of 33 Ohms was introduced to the circuit. This resistor converts the secondary current into a voltage drop suitable for the input of the ADS1115 analog-to-digital converter. The relationship between the Root Mean Square current flowing through the load and the peak voltage detected by the sensor is calculated using Equation \ref{eq:peak_voltage}.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:peak_voltage}
V_{peak} = \sqrt{2} \times I_{RMS(secondary)} \times R_{burden}
\end{equation}
This calibration ensures that for a standard load, the signal remains within the dynamic range of the 16-bit ADS1115, which was interfaced via the I2C bus to digitize the analog waveforms. The physical implementation of the prototype was validated under active load conditions to confirm correct end-to-end sensing and data acquisition prior to extended testing. While this configuration successfully demonstrated the feasibility of the software architecture and edge-processing capabilities, the system exhibited high idle power consumption (523 mA) and significant unit cost, necessitating the subsequent optimization phases.

\subsection{Second Generation: Embedded Optimization and Integrated Metrology}
\label{subsec:gen2}

Following the functional validation of the first generation, the hardware architecture was significantly revised to address two primary deficiencies identified during the proof-of-concept phase: the high idle power consumption (523 mA) of the First Generation quad-core processor and the calibration instability associated with discrete analog sensors. Consequently, the second generation architecture was designed around the Raspberry Pi Zero 2W\cite{RPiZeroW_Datasheet} and the STMicroelectronics STPM33 \cite{STPM33_Datasheet} metering Application Specific Standard Product (ASSP).

The transition to the Raspberry Pi Zero 2W was motivated by the requirement to minimize the thermal footprint and energy overhead of the edge node. Unlike the Raspberry Pi 3 Model B, the Zero 2W employs a Quad-core 64-bit ARM Cortex-A53 processor clocked at 1GHz with 512MB of SDRAM. This architectural reduction results in a 74\% decrease in idle power consumption (134 mA) while retaining sufficient computational capacity for the embedded Linux environment. Furthermore, the inclusion of onboard Wi-Fi (802.11 b/g/n) and Bluetooth in the 'W' model facilitated direct connectivity to the Head-End System without external modems, further reducing the physical footprint of the device. The integration of these components, including the interface between the SoC and the peripheral tampering and display modules, is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:gen2_hardware}.

Simultaneously, the metrology subsystem was upgraded from discrete transformer-based sensing to a dedicated integrated circuit. The STPM33 was selected for its ability to perform hardware-based calculation of active, reactive, and apparent energy, significantly exceeding the linearity achievable by the previous discrete configuration. The STPM33 interfaces with the host processor via the Serial Peripheral Interface (SPI) bus, which was prioritized over UART to ensure higher data throughput for real-time waveform acquisition. The electrical interface between the grid and the metering IC required specific signal conditioning to match the input dynamic range of the STPM33. Voltage sensing is achieved through a resistive voltage divider network composed of an 810 k$\Omega$ primary resistor (R1) and a 470 $\Omega$ secondary resistor (R2) \cite{EVALSTPM33_UserManual}. To prevent high-frequency noise from corrupting the measurements, an anti-aliasing filter was implemented using a 22 nF capacitor in parallel with the secondary resistor for the voltage channel. Similarly, the current sensing path, driven by Current Transformers (CTs) with a sensitivity of 2.4 mV/A, incorporates a 10 nF capacitor and 1 k$\Omega$ resistors to filter the input signal before digitization \cite{EVALSTPM33_UserManual}. \subsection{Third Generation: Industrial Scalability and Custom Fabrication}
\label{subsec:gen3}

The third generation of the NGSM architecture was developed to transition the system from a functional prototype to an industrially viable product. This iteration focuses on high-density integration, supply chain independence, and software reliability suitable for mass deployment. The core of this architecture is a custom-fabricated Printed Circuit Board (PCB) design centered around the Allwinner F1C200s System on Chip (SoC) \cite{f1c200s}.

The selection of the Allwinner F1C200s was driven by the necessity for a highly integrated embedded controller capable of supporting multimedia interfaces while maintaining a minimal hardware footprint. The F1C200s (implemented on the MangoPi R3 board \cite{mangopi}) features an ARM926EJ-S core clocked at 420 MHz and integrates 64MB of DDR1 memory directly within the SoC package. This System-in-Package (SiP) design eliminates the need for external RAM modules, significantly reducing the Bill of Materials (BOM) complexity and minimizing trace length susceptibility to electromagnetic interference (EMI) in industrial environments. Furthermore, the SoC natively supports the parallel RGB interface required for the TFT display and the SPI bus for high-speed communication with the metrology unit, allowing for a streamlined hardware topology.

To ensure long-term operational reliability, the storage architecture was migrated from removable SD cards to an onboard SPI NAND Flash memory chip. This change addresses the data integrity risks associated with mechanical contact failure and file system corruption common in consumer-grade storage media. The software environment was strictly optimized through a custom embedded Linux deployment generated via the Buildroot automation system. To utilize the F1C200s effectively, the Device Tree Source (DTS) was manually reconfigured. Conflicting multimedia modules, specifically the LCD controller, TV encoder, and Camera interface, were disabled to release system resources and interrupt lines. This customization enabled the dedication of the high-speed hardware SPI1 controller to the metrology interface on Port E, ensuring reliable communication with the STPM33. Furthermore, the bootloader sequence was customized using U-Boot with specific offsets to support the proprietary partition table. For connectivity, the kernel capabilities were expanded to support USB On-The-Go (OTG) and RNDIS Ethernet gadget modes, facilitating secure local SSH access for system maintenance without requiring external network infrastructure.

As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:firmware_stack}, this high-efficiency firmware stack separates kernel-space drivers from user-space services. The kernel adheres to a monolithic design where essential drivers are built-in, avoiding the overhead of loadable modules. Core meter functions are implemented as background daemons, specifically utilizing the specialized hardware SPI and GPIO mappings defined in the custom Device Tree to interact with the sensors. Inter-process communication is used to decouple data acquisition from visualization and network tasks, allowing failures in non-critical services to avoid disrupting continuous metrology.

\subsection{Power Quality and Harmonic Analysis}
\label{subsec:harmonics}

Power quality health serves as a fundamental indicator of electrical system stability. In modern distribution networks, the proliferation of non-linear loads such as Switch-Mode Power Supplies (SMPS), Variable Frequency Drives (VFDs), and household electronics has introduced significant challenges to maintaining a sinusoidal waveform \cite{dugan2012electrical}. These loads draw non-sinusoidal currents, resulting in harmonic distortions that can lead to equipment overheating, neutral conductor overloading, and mechanical resonance in transformers. Recent studies emphasize the necessity of robust classification methods to identify these disturbances early and prevent long-term infrastructure damage \cite{eristi2022new}. Consequently, the ability to detect and quantify these disturbances at the grid edge is a critical function of the NGSM system. Harmonics are defined as voltage or current components operating at frequencies that are integer multiples of the fundamental system frequency. According to Fourier's theorem, any periodic non-sinusoidal waveform can be decomposed into the summation of a DC component, a fundamental sine wave, and a series of higher-order harmonic frequencies. The instantaneous signal $y(t)$ is modeled as shown in Equation \ref{eq:fourier}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fourier}
y(t) = Y_0 + \sum_{h=1}^{\infty} Y_h \sqrt{2} \sin(h\omega t - \phi_h)
\end{equation}

Where $Y_0$ represents the DC component, $Y_h$ is the Root Mean Square (RMS) value of the harmonic at order $h$, $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the fundamental component, and $\phi_h$ is the phase displacement \cite{wakileh2001power}.

To quantify the severity of the waveform distortion, the Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) metric is utilized. This indicator expresses the ratio of the RMS value of all harmonic components to the RMS value of the fundamental frequency, calculated via Equation \ref{eq:thd}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:thd}
THD = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_{h=2}^{H} Y_h^2}}{Y_1}
\end{equation}

The NGSM architecture implements a distributed detection strategy that processes these waveforms directly at the meter node. By utilizing the high-frequency sampling capabilities of the metrology unit, the embedded firmware executes a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to extract the spectral composition of the voltage and current signals. This approach aligns with modern distributed edge computing paradigms, which prioritize local data processing to achieve efficient and adaptable grid management without incurring the latency associated with centralized cloud analysis \cite{shang2021achieving}.

To ensure accurate spectral estimation and minimize spectral leakage caused by finite windowing of non-periodic signals, a Blackman-Harris window function is applied to the time-domain signal prior to the FFT computation. The fundamental frequency is identified by detecting the peak magnitude index in the frequency spectrum. The RMS amplitude of the fundamental component ($Y_1$) and each subsequent harmonic order ($Y_h$) are then extracted. The magnitude of each harmonic is resolved relative to the fundamental to provide a normalized measure of distortion distinct from absolute signal amplitude.

The THD monitoring and alerting logic follows harmonic distortion guidance from the IEEE 519-2022 standard \cite{ieee519_2022}. In the proposed NGSM prototype, the required voltage and current measurements are acquired through the STPM33 metering front-end and then are used to compute harmonic indicators for power-quality supervision at the grid edge. Voltage THD ($THD_V$) is prioritized using threshold-based levels derived from IEEE 519-2022 \cite{ieee519_2022}: values below 5\% are treated as nominal, while values above 8\% raise a high-priority alert indicating potentially harmful distortion conditions. In the same manner, current THD ($THD_I$) is monitored to detect major load-induced distortions. Figure \ref{fig:thd_analysis} illustrates a representative case from the STPM33-based prototype, contrasting a heavily distorted current waveform ($THD_I \approx 35.50\%$) with a compliant voltage waveform ($THD_V \approx 2.16\%$), as shown in the side-by-side comparison.

\subsection{Energy Demand Forecasting}
\label{subsec:forecasting}

To support proactive grid management and load balancing, the NGSM analytics suite integrates a comparative forecasting framework for short-horizon energy demand prediction. Energy demand forecasting estimates future consumption from historical measurements and is an enabling capability for cost-aware and carbon-aware operation in modern smart grids~\cite{Alsamraee2025}. While accurate forecasting is often challenged by non-stationary usage patterns, household behavior variability, and latent exogenous factors~\cite{Hossain2025}, this work evaluates three representative modeling paradigms, a classical statistical baseline, a deep recurrent network, and an attention-based architecture to characterize their suitability for univariate residential load forecasting.

\subsubsection{Dataset Description (UK-DALE)}
All experiments are conducted using the UK Domestic Appliance-Level Electricity dataset (UK-DALE)~\cite{Kelly2015ukdale}. UK-DALE provides long-duration residential electricity measurements (including whole-home demand and appliance-level sub-metering) collected from multiple UK homes. In this study the focus is on a univariate forecasting setting and use the aggregated active power signal (``Active Power'') as the prediction target, consistent with the NGSM objective of modeling demand at the meter/aggregate level rather than per-appliance disaggregation.

\subsubsection{Preprocessing and Data Protocol}
The raw signal is indexed by timestamps. Timestamps are converted into timezone-free datetime objects and use them as the temporal index to ensure consistent time ordering and deterministic slicing of the series. To ensure a fixed and uniform temporal resolution, the series is resampled to a regular 1-second frequency (1s) using mean aggregation and linear interpolation for missing values, and boundary NaNs are removed.

Let $N$ denote the total number of samples after resampling. To form a deterministic forecasting benchmark, the training set is constructed from the first quarter of the available series, i.e., indices $[0, \lfloor N/4 \rfloor)$. The test set is constructed from the immediately following fixed window of length $T=2{,}500{,}000$ samples, i.e., indices $[\lfloor N/4 \rfloor, \lfloor N/4 \rfloor + T)$. Since the resampled resolution is 1 second, this evaluation horizon corresponds to a long contiguous duration on the order of weeks, enabling assessment under prolonged distribution shifts and rare operating regimes.

To prevent information leakage, a Min--Max scaler is fitted only on the training portion and apply the learned scaling parameters to both training and test segments. For all models, predictions are inverse-transformed to the original active power scale prior to reporting error metrics.

\subsubsection{Forecasting Models}

As a classical baseline capturing linear temporal dependencies, an Auto-Regressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model was implemented (ARIMA) model with order $(p,d,q)=(1,1,0)$. The ARIMA model is fitted on the scaled training sequence. During inference, the model produces a contiguous forecast spanning the full test horizon, enabling direct comparison with neural forecasting models on identical test indices.

To model nonlinear temporal structure, a stacked Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network was trained in a sequence-to-one configuration. The model consumes a look-back window of length $L=1024$ and predicts the next value. The architecture follows a stacked design with three LSTM blocks of hidden sizes $(64,32,16)$, with Batch Normalization and Dropout ($p=0.2$) to improve generalization, followed by a dense output projection. Optimization is performed using Nadam with MSE loss. The model is trained for 20 epochs with batch size 64 and uses validation split of 0.2.

To assess an attention-based alternative for long-range dependency modeling, a Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) was implemented using the Darts library and trained it on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. The training pipeline uses PyTorch Lightning with automatic mixed precision (bf16-mixed) and early stopping based on validation loss. A validation series is created by splitting the training sequence at 90\% (trainseries.split before(0.9)), enabling deterministic monitoring of generalization during training. The TFT configuration includes: hidden size 64, one LSTM layer (lstm layers=1), four attention heads (num attention heads=4), dropout 0.3, and batch size 2048, with a maximum of 15 epochs and early stopping patience of 3. The model is checkpointed and exported for reproducible reloading and evaluation.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocol}
A key requirement in this benchmark is that all models are evaluated under the same forecasting task definition. MAE, RMSE, and MAPE was computed on the same contiguous test horizon of length $T=2{,}500{,}000$ samples after inverse scaling.

For TFT, evaluation is performed via rolling one-step-ahead forecasting using Darts historical forecasts. The model generates next-step predictions with (forecast horizon=1) and (stride=1), without retraining (retrain=False). The parameter (last points only=True) is used to return one prediction per step and reduce memory overhead. Predicted timestamps are intersected with the ground-truth timestamps to ensure strict temporal alignment prior to metric computation.

\subsection{Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring}
\label{subsec:nilm}

Non-Intrusive Load Monitoring (NILM) enables appliance-level energy disaggregation from aggregate power measurements, providing granular consumption insights without installing sensors on individual devices. This subsection presents a comprehensive NILM framework based on an Enhanced Dilated Temporal Convolutional Network (EDTCN) architecture, evaluated on three public datasets under a documented protocol and optimized for embedded deployment.

\textbf{Task Formulation:} The framework addresses two related tasks: (1) \textit{Regression} -- estimating the continuous power of every appliance in Watts (evaluated with MAE, RMSE, SAE, NDE and energy accuracy), and (2) \textit{State detection} -- deciding whether each appliance is ON (evaluated with precision, recall and F1). The regression network has one output head per appliance and is trained with a regression loss only; there is no classification term in the regression objective. ON/OFF states are obtained by thresholding the estimated power at 15~W (the same threshold is applied to the ground truth). A separate classification network with the same backbone is trained with a weighted binary cross-entropy for the comparison in Section~\ref{subsubsec:classification}. All F1, precision and recall values in this paper are computed from the regression outputs unless explicitly labeled as ``Classification Model'' results.

\subsubsection{Enhanced EDTCN architecture}
\label{subsubsec:dtcn_architecture}

The proposed architecture extends the baseline Temporal Convolutional Network with deeper layers, wider channels, and exponentially increasing dilation factors to capture long-range temporal dependencies in power consumption patterns. The model consists of three primary components: input projection, stacked residual TCN blocks, and per-appliance output heads.

The core dilated convolution operation is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dilated_conv}
F(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \cdot x_{t - d \cdot k},
\end{equation}

where $K$ is the kernel size, $w_k$ are the convolution weights, and $d$ is the dilation factor. Each residual TCN block applies two dilated convolutions (kernel size $K=3$, dilation $d_i$, padding $(K-1)d_i$, output trimmed to the input length), each followed by batch normalization, ReLU and dropout ($p=0.2$), and adds the block input through an identity (or $1{\times}1$) skip connection. Six blocks with exponentially increasing dilations $d_i\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}$ follow an input projection ($\mathrm{Conv1d}_{1\rightarrow128}$, kernel 7). A dilated convolution with kernel $K$ and dilation $d$ extends the receptive field by $(K-1)d$ steps, so the two convolutions of block $i$ add $2(K-1)d_i$ steps and the receptive field of the complete network is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rf}
R = 1+(k_{in}-1)+2(K-1)\sum_{i=1}^{6} d_i = 1+6+2\cdot 2\cdot 63 = 259\ \text{steps},
\end{equation}
as derived block by block in Table~\ref{tab:rf}. All three datasets are resampled to $\Delta t=8$~s (Section~\ref{subsubsec:datasets}), so $R\,\Delta t = 2072$~s $=34.5$ minutes of history, and the 512-step input window spans 68.3 minutes. For another sampling interval the span scales linearly with $\Delta t$ (4.3 minutes at 1~s, 25.9 minutes at 6~s, 43.2 minutes at 10~s).

Given an aggregate sequence $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times1\times T}$ with $T=512$, the input projection gives $\mathbf{h}_0=\mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{BN}(\mathrm{Conv1d}_{1\rightarrow128}(\mathbf{x}))))$, each residual block computes $\mathbf{h}_i=\mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(F_i(\mathbf{h}_{i-1})+\mathbf{h}_{i-1}))$ with $F_i$ the two dilated convolution--normalization stages, and the $A$ heads map the shared representation $\mathbf{h}_6$ to the per-appliance power $\mathbf{y}_a=\mathrm{Conv1d}_{32\rightarrow1}(\mathrm{Dropout}(\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{Conv1d}_{128\rightarrow32}(\mathbf{h}_6))))$, giving the output $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T\times A}$. The shared trunk learns temporal features common to all appliances (cycles, transients, baseline variations) and the independent heads specialize in appliance-specific signatures. The exponential dilation schedule realizes a hierarchy of temporal scales: shallow blocks resolve start-up transients, deep blocks aggregate cycle-level context such as washing-machine program phases.

\subsubsection{Training Strategy and Loss Functions}
\label{subsubsec:training_strategy}

The framework employs two specialized models trained independently: a regression model for continuous power prediction and a classification model for binary ON/OFF state detection. This separation addresses the inherent conflict between minimizing power estimation error (MAE) and maximizing state detection accuracy (F1-score).

\paragraph{Regression Model} The regression model predicts per-appliance power with a device-type-weighted focal loss that targets three NILM difficulties: (1) appliance power scales spanning two orders of magnitude (2~W laptops versus 2000~W kettles), (2) temporal imbalance (burst devices are active only a few percent of the time) and (3) the need to concentrate learning on samples with large errors. With the absolute error $e_{i,t,a}=|\hat{y}_{i,t,a}-y_{i,t,a}|$ and the normalized error $p_{i,t,a}=\min\!\big(1,\,e_{i,t,a}/\max(|y_{i,t,a}|,\,y_{\min})\big)$ with $y_{\min}=1$~W, the loss is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:focal_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{focal} = \frac{1}{N\,T\,A}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{a=1}^{A} w_{\tau(a)}\; p_{i,t,a}^{\,\gamma}\,\Big(\alpha\, e_{i,t,a}^{2} + (1-\alpha)\, e_{i,t,a}\Big),
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the batch size, $T=512$ the window length, $A$ the number of appliances, $\tau(a)\in\{A,B,C\}$ the usage type of appliance $a$, $w_\tau$ its weight, $\gamma$ the focusing exponent and $\alpha$ the weight of the squared-error term. The floor $y_{\min}$ and the clipping to 1 bound the modulation factor $p^{\gamma}$ to $[0,1]$ in every appliance state (for an OFF appliance $p=\min(1,e/1\,\mathrm{W})$); $p^{\gamma}$ is the regression analogue of the $(1-p_t)^{\gamma}$ factor of classification focal loss, down-weighting samples that are already predicted well and giving full weight to samples whose error is comparable to the signal. The loss is a pure regression objective on a single output per appliance (one head per appliance, Section~\ref{subsubsec:dtcn_architecture}); the quadratic--linear mix combines the strong penalty of MSE on high-power events with the outlier robustness of MAE. The loss is averaged over batch, time and appliances and contains no threshold; the 15~W ON/OFF threshold is only used to compute state metrics.

The type weights are fixed priors, not learned: $w_A=w_B=1.5$ for always-on/cycling (type A) and regular-use (type B) appliances, and $w_C=2.0$ for burst appliances (type C), i.e. larger weights for appliances that are active more rarely and more abruptly, with $\alpha=0.7$ and $\gamma=1.0$ (``Config 3''). Three configurations differing in $\gamma$ and in the weights were compared on REFIT and yielded MAE values of 15.66, 16.09 and 17.24~W (Config 3, 2 and 1), i.e. a spread of at most 1.6~W, showing that the model is not sensitive to these choices within the tested range. The same setting is used on all datasets without per-dataset tuning. The contribution of the loss relative to MSE, MAE, standard focal loss and type-weighted MSE is quantified in the ablation study of Section~\ref{subsubsec:ablation}.

\paragraph{Classification Model} The classification head predicts binary ON/OFF states using weighted binary cross-entropy:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bce_loss}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_{BCE} &= -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{a=1}^{A} \Big[ w_{pos,a} \cdot y_{i,a}^{binary} \cdot \log(\sigma(\hat{y}_{i,a})) \\
&\quad + (1 - y_{i,a}^{binary}) \cdot \log(1 - \sigma(\hat{y}_{i,a})) \Big],
\end{split}
\end{equation}

where $y_{i,a}^{binary} = \mathbb{1}[y_{i,a} > \tau]$ is the binary ground truth derived by thresholding power at $\tau=15$W, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, and $w_{pos,a}$ is the per-appliance positive class weight computed as the ratio of negative to positive samples: $w_{pos,a} = (1 - r_a) / r_a$, where $r_a$ is the ON-time ratio for appliance $a$. This adaptive weighting prevents the trivial ``always-OFF'' solution for appliances with low duty cycles.

The deployed checkpoints were trained with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine annealing with warm restarts ($T_0=10$, $T_{mult}=2$, $\eta_{min}=10^{-6}$), gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, batch size 4096, up to 100 epochs and early stopping with a patience of 25 epochs on the validation loss. Inputs are windows of 512 aggregate-power samples at 8-second resolution (Section~\ref{subsubsec:datasets}).

\paragraph{Training Efficiency} Pre-converting the arrays to tensors, enabling cuDNN auto-tuning and reducing per-batch logging cut the epoch time on the 380K-window REFIT training set from 16 to 2.5 minutes on an NVIDIA A100 (6.4$\times$ speed-up, Figure~\ref{fig:training_speedup}), which made the search over loss configurations practical.

\paragraph{Controlled experiments and reproducibility} The ablation, baseline, robustness and adaptation experiments retrain every model under one protocol: Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cosine annealing to 1\% of the initial rate, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, mixed-precision (FP16) training, batch size 512, at most 100 epochs for SustDataED2 and UK-DALE and 25 epochs for REFIT (reduced protocol: every third training window), early stopping with a patience of 25 epochs on the validation loss, and checkpoint selection by validation loss only; the test split is evaluated once. Seeds 2026--2030 are used for SustDataED2 and UK-DALE (three seeds for the EDTCN on REFIT, two for the REFIT baselines). Training ran with PyTorch 2.6 (CUDA 12.4) on an NVIDIA RTX 3050 6~GB laptop GPU (150 training runs, about 31 hours in total). Confidence intervals use a block bootstrap over 32-window blocks (1000 resamples), and differences between models are tested with a paired block bootstrap on identical test windows.

\paragraph{Preprocessing} The same pipeline is applied to all datasets. (i) \textit{Resampling:} every channel is resampled to a regular 8-second grid by averaging, and gaps shorter than one minute (up to 7 samples) are forward-filled. (ii) \textit{Missing values:} in SustDataED2 the remaining gaps are set to 0~W (appliance not drawing power); in UK-DALE windows with more than 10\% missing samples are discarded and the remainder zero-filled. (iii) \textit{Synchronization:} the SustDataED2 appliance files are logged by independent sensors, so timestamps are rounded to whole seconds before resampling (Section~\ref{subsubsec:SustDataED2_preprocessing}). (iv) \textit{Windowing:} 512-step windows, non-overlapping for SustDataED2 and UK-DALE and with a 256-step stride for REFIT; since splits are by household (REFIT) or chronological (SustDataED2, UK-DALE), overlapping windows never straddle two splits. SustDataED2 windows in which more than half of the aggregate samples are below 1~W are removed. (v) \textit{Normalization:} the aggregate is standardized with the mean and standard deviation of the training split; targets stay in Watts. (vi) \textit{Thresholds and activation filtering:} no activation filtering is applied to the training data; the 15~W ON/OFF threshold is used only to compute state metrics, and the optional post-processing threshold is selected on the validation split (Section~\ref{subsubsec:threshold_optimization}). (vii) \textit{Voltage systems:} only active power is used, so differences in mains voltage between datasets do not enter the model. The SustDataED2 aggregate is the sum of the twelve metered appliance channels (a closed-system setting without unmetered load), whereas REFIT and UK-DALE use the measured mains signal, which contains unmetered consumption; this difference is one reason for the lower error on SustDataED2.

\paragraph{SustDataED2 (primary)} The Smart University of Science and Technology dataset contains 12 appliances with raw sampling intervals of 1--6~s. After resampling to 8~s and windowing, the chronological 70/15/15\% split yields 1,416, 303 and 304 windows. The timestamp irregularities handled in Section~\ref{subsubsec:SustDataED2_preprocessing} are part of the preprocessing.

Not every house contains every appliance, so per-house evaluations score only the appliances physically present.

\subsubsection{Multi-Dataset Performance}
\label{subsubsec:performance}

\subsubsection{Ablation Study}
\label{subsubsec:ablation}

To isolate the contribution of each design choice, ten variants were trained with identical data, seeds, optimizer, epochs and validation-based checkpoint selection (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Differences are tested with a paired block bootstrap on identical test windows, averaged over the five seeds.

\textit{Architecture.} Dilation is the decisive ingredient. Starting from a plain 6-block TCN, adding exponentially dilated convolutions with the 6-block receptive field (EDTCN with the same MSE+MAE loss) lowers the MAE from 5.58 to 4.99~W on SustDataED2 (11\%) and from 16.22 to 14.29~W on UK-DALE (12\%), and raises the F1 from 0.746 to 0.830 and from 0.534 to 0.560. Residual connections need dilation to be useful: without it they do not improve the plain TCN (6.61 and 17.54~W) and make training less stable (F1 standard deviation 0.18 across seeds on SustDataED2), whereas the dilated residual blocks train reliably. Extending the receptive field from 67 to 259 steps (4 to 6 blocks) has no measurable effect on SustDataED2 (4.92 versus 4.99~W) but reduces the UK-DALE error from 15.48 to 14.29~W, in line with the long cycles (washing machine, dishwasher) of that dataset.

\textit{Loss function.} With the 6-block EDTCN fixed, the losses behave differently along two axes. MAE as the training loss gives the lowest MAE (4.68~W and 13.51~W) but systematically underestimates the energy: its SAE is 0.46 and 0.54, about 2.5 times that of the other losses (0.18--0.25), with high precision and low recall. The standard focal loss ($\gamma=2$) is the weakest objective on both datasets. The remaining losses (MSE+MAE, MSE, type-weighted MSE and the proposed device-type-weighted focal loss) lie within 0.4~W (SustDataED2) and 0.8~W (UK-DALE) of each other. Within the focal family, the device-type weights help on UK-DALE (15.01 versus 15.25~W without weights) and are neutral on SustDataED2. Compared with plain MSE+MAE the proposed loss has a slightly higher MAE, but it changes the operating point of the state detector: Table~\ref{tab:loss_by_type} shows that it raises recall in all but one of the eight dataset--type groups, most clearly for regular-use appliances on REFIT (recall 0.52 to 0.71, F1 0.28 to 0.36) and for burst appliances on SustDataED2 (0.38 to 0.46), while F1 stays within 0.01 elsewhere. The loss is therefore best understood as a recall-oriented training objective for event-driven services (demand response, activity detection) that can be selected or re-weighted according to the application, whereas the accuracy gain of the EDTCN over prior architectures comes from the dilated residual architecture itself.

\subsubsection{Comparison with Baselines under an Identical Protocol}
\label{subsubsec:sota_comparison}

Overall MAE values reported in the literature depend on the appliance set, the window length, the sampling interval and the train/test split, so numbers copied from different papers are not comparable and are deliberately not tabulated here. Instead, Seq2Point~\cite{Kelly2018}, UNet-NILM~\cite{Bonfigli2019} and BERT4NILM~\cite{Yue2020} were re-implemented as compact networks with the same interface (512-step aggregate input, one output sequence per appliance) and trained exactly like the EDTCN: same windows and splits, optimizer, epoch budget, seeds, validation-based checkpoint selection and the MSE+MAE loss. They are our own re-implementations and not the original code or weights, and they were not individually tuned. Table~\ref{tab:baselines} and Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline_comparison} show the result. On every dataset the EDTCN has a lower MAE and a higher F1 than every baseline; with the deployed loss the MAE is 26\% (SustDataED2), 9\% (REFIT) and 11\% (UK-DALE) lower than that of the strongest baseline (BERT4NILM-style in all three cases), and with the matched MSE+MAE loss 31\%, 12\% and 16\%. All differences are significant in the paired test ($p<0.001$) and are many times larger than the seed-to-seed standard deviation.

The EDTCN is also the most computationally demanding of the compared networks (377.8~M MACs versus 50.0~M, 75.2~M and 84.8~M for the baselines), which is the price of its long receptive field. The ablation shows how this cost can be traded: the 4-block variant needs 263~M MACs (30\% fewer) and 508,745 parameters, and with the MSE+MAE loss it still reaches 4.92~W on SustDataED2 and 15.48~W on UK-DALE, below the strongest baseline on both datasets (7.23 and 16.95~W). The on-device latency of the deployed model is reported in Section~\ref{subsubsec:deployment_details}.

\subsubsection{The False Positive Problem in Regression-Based NILM}
\label{subsubsec:false_positive_problem}

A critical limitation discovered during evaluation is that regression models optimized for Mean Absolute Error exhibit a systematic bias toward predicting small positive power values when appliances are OFF, the ``slightly-ON-everywhere'' phenomenon. This fundamental problem stems from the mathematical structure of the MAE objective: since MAE penalizes false positives (predicting ON when appliance is OFF) and false negatives (predicting OFF when appliance is ON) equally, the model learns to hedge its predictions rather than commit to confident binary ON/OFF decisions. Specifically, predicting 10W for an OFF appliance (true power = 0W) incurs only 10W penalty, while confidently predicting 0W when the appliance unexpectedly turns ON (true power = 500W) incurs a catastrophic 500W penalty. To minimize expected loss, the model rationally adopts a conservative strategy of predicting small positive values everywhere.

This behavior is quantified on the unseen REFIT test houses: at the 15~W state threshold the unprocessed regression output of the deployed model has a pooled precision of 30.6\% (all nine appliances and all 59,927 test windows) together with a pooled recall of 83.8\% and a MAE of 15.66~W, i.e. most activations are found but 69.4\% of the ON decisions are false alarms. The per-appliance breakdown shows where they come from: the microwave reaches only 5.8\% precision, the computer 6.7\%, the washing machine 12.8\% and the tumble dryer 13.8\%, whereas the fridge (54.0\%) and the kettle (19.4\%) are much cleaner. The reason is the ``ghost'' output of 5--15~W that the model emits during OFF periods of rarely used appliances: it is too small to affect the MAE appreciably but sufficient to cross the 15~W threshold when it persists over many time steps.

This observation has a methodological consequence that applies to NILM evaluation in general: MAE alone cannot reveal this failure mode, because a model can have a good MAE and still produce a state signal that is not directly usable. For this reason all experiments in this paper report MAE together with RMSE, SAE, NDE, precision, recall and F1 for every appliance of every dataset, including the appliances that are not detected (for example the SustDataED2 vacuum cleaner and the UK-DALE laptop and router, F1 = 0). We recommend that multi-metric, per-appliance reporting become the norm in NILM comparisons.

\paragraph{Cross-dataset transfer and adaptation protocol} A source EDTCN with the proposed loss was trained only on the five appliances that exist in both REFIT and UK-DALE (fridge, washing machine, dishwasher, microwave, kettle) using source data (two seeds), once with REFIT and once with UK-DALE as the source. The target households are UK-DALE house 1 and the REFIT test houses 19, 20 and 21. Three situations are evaluated on a held-out block of target data: \textit{zero-shot} use of the source model (the target aggregate is converted to the input convention of the source model); \textit{adaptation} with the first $k$ days of the target household, $k=1,3,7$ (one 512-step window spans 68 minutes, so 21, 63 and 148 windows); and training from scratch on the same $k$ days. For UK-DALE the adaptation data are the first windows of the chronologically earlier training split, for a REFIT house they are the first windows of that house; the next 300 windows are used for validation and the remaining later windows form the evaluation block, which is identical for every $k$. REFIT windows overlap by 50\%, so one window is left out between the blocks, and an exact-duplicate check between adaptation, validation and evaluation windows found no shared window in any of the 24 configurations. The adaptation methods are \textit{heads only} (the trunk is frozen and the appliance heads are trained, learning rate $3\cdot10^{-4}$), \textit{full fine-tuning} (all parameters, learning rate $10^{-4}$) and \textit{full fine-tuning with elastic weight consolidation} (EWC), which adds the penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_i(\theta_i-\theta_i^{*})^2$ with the diagonal Fisher information $F_i$ of the source model (estimated on 640 source windows and normalized to unit mean) and the source weights $\theta^{*}$. The penalty weight $\lambda\in\{10^2,\dots,10^7\}$ is chosen on the validation block as the largest value whose validation loss is within 5\% of the best one (the minimum would always select the weakest penalty). All adaptation runs use 40 epochs, a batch of at most 64 windows and the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. Adaptation is computed offline on a PC or a cloud server; the meter only receives the exported INT8 model and performs inference.

\paragraph{Results} Table~\ref{tab:transfer} summarizes the experiments. In the zero-shot setting, the source models detect the states of the target appliances with an F1 of 0.48--0.57, which is 86--88\% of the F1 of the in-domain models (0.56--0.66) although they have never seen a sample of the target household. The MAE is less informative in this setting because the appliances are OFF most of the time: predicting zero for all five appliances gives a MAE of 12--24~W, of the same order as (and for several houses below) the zero-shot MAE, and the F1 is therefore the more relevant indicator. Adaptation then lowers the MAE: with seven days of data the best adapted model improves the zero-shot MAE by 1.6~W on UK-DALE and by 0.2, 2.2 and 6.0~W on REFIT houses 19, 20 and 21, while the F1 stays at 0.50--0.63. Training from scratch on the same days is clearly worse (F1 between 0.05 and 0.53 and a MAE up to 17~W above the adapted models), which shows that the source model carries most of the useful knowledge and that a few days are sufficient to specialize it; heads-only adaptation is the safest option for one day of data, whereas full fine-tuning or EWC pays off at seven days.

Full fine-tuning has a price: the MAE on the source dataset increases by 3--10~W (catastrophic forgetting). With one or three days of target data, EWC reduces this forgetting by up to 57\% at equal or better target accuracy, for example on REFIT house 20 with one day of data (forgetting 5.3 $\rightarrow$ 2.3~W, MAE 15.7 $\rightarrow$ 15.3~W) and on house 21 (MAE 29.4 $\rightarrow$ 27.7~W with one day and 32.0 $\rightarrow$ 28.2~W with three days). With seven days of data the validation rule selects a weaker penalty, the reduction of forgetting is small (at most 12\%), and the benefit of EWC is mostly in the target accuracy of house 21 (30.5 $\rightarrow$ 26.1~W). EWC therefore limits but does not remove forgetting, and a strong penalty restricts adaptation when the target differs most from the source. Freezing the trunk limits the change of the source MAE to at most 2.7~W. Adaptation does not help every appliance: the dishwasher of UK-DALE, which runs for only about 2\% of the time and therefore contributes few cycles to a week of data, has a zero-shot MAE of 18.1~W and an adapted MAE between 18.5 and 24.0~W, so rarely used appliances are best served by a larger source model or by longer adaptation periods.

\paragraph{Behavior over time} The test split of each dataset is chronological. Dividing it into four consecutive quarters (UK-DALE test period of about four months), the MAE of the retrained models is 5.5, 5.5, 4.9 and 5.2~W on SustDataED2 and 14.8, 14.9, 16.7 and 13.6~W on UK-DALE, without a monotonic degradation within these periods. \subsubsection{SustDataED2 Dataset Preprocessing Challenge}
\label{subsubsec:SustDataED2_preprocessing}

The twelve SustDataED2 appliance files are logged by independent sensors whose clocks differ by microseconds (for example 10:00:00.123456 versus 10:00:00.234567 for the same nominal second), which makes timestamp-based joins fail. Rounding every timestamp to the nearest second before the 8-second resampling (\texttt{dt.round('1s')}) resolves this: all twelve appliances then share identical indices and can be merged into the aggregate signal, while the original sampling intervals of 1--6~s remain finer than the 8~s grid, so no temporal information that matters at this resolution is lost. Multi-sensor datasets recorded with independent loggers frequently have this problem, and documenting the fix supports reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Embedded Deployment Pipeline}
\label{subsubsec:deployment_details}

Transitioning deep learning models from GPU training environments to resource-constrained embedded platforms requires systematic optimization beyond quantization alone. The complete deployment pipeline for the F1C200s (ARM926EJ-S, 64MB RAM, no FPU) comprised four sequential stages designed to progressively reduce model size and computational requirements while maintaining inferential accuracy.

\paragraph{Stage 1: Graph Optimization via PNNX}
The PyTorch computation graph was converted to the PNNX (PyTorch Neural Network eXchange) intermediate representation, which performs high-level operator fusion to eliminate redundant operations. Standard ONNX runtimes preserve individual operations for maximum compatibility, but PNNX leverages PyTorch-specific semantics to merge consecutive operations (e.g., Conv + BatchNorm + ReLU) into single fused kernels. This fusion reduces memory access overhead--the dominant bottleneck on CPU-only systems--by minimizing intermediate activation tensor writes to DRAM. The PNNX output was subsequently converted to NCNN format, a lightweight inference framework optimized for mobile and embedded deployment.

\paragraph{Stage 2: Calibration for Quantization Range Determination}
Post-training quantization requires representative data to determine optimal clipping ranges for weights and activations. A calibration set of about 500 windows was drawn from the validation split (half selected by appliance activity, half evenly spaced), so that ON and OFF states of all appliances are represented. Activation ranges were determined by minimizing the Kullback--Leibler divergence $D_{KL}(P_{fp32}\,\|\,P_{quantized}(T))$ between the FP32 activation histogram and the histogram after clipping to $[-T,T]$ and quantizing, which selects the threshold $T$ that loses the least information; the analytical ACIQ clipping criterion was evaluated as an alternative. The NCNN quantization tools handle only 2-D convolution layers, whereas the network consists of 1-D convolutions that the converter exports as a separate layer type and the tools silently skip; the 1-D convolutions were therefore expressed as exactly equivalent $(1\times k)$ 2-D convolutions with the same weights (the outputs are identical to the original network), after which every convolution can be calibrated and quantized.

\paragraph{Stage 3: INT8 Post-Training Quantization}
Weights were quantized per output channel and activations per tensor with the symmetric mapping
\begin{equation}
x_{int8} = \text{clip}(\text{round}(x_{fp32} / s), -128, 127)
\end{equation}
using the scale factors $s$ from the calibration table. The deployed INT8 models store the weights in 0.72--0.98~MB, 3.9$\times$ smaller than the FP32 weights (Table~\ref{tab:quantization_results}). Table~\ref{tab:int8_pc} evaluates the true INT8 models on the complete test splits on a PC. For the regression models the accuracy is essentially preserved: with ACIQ clipping the MAE changes by -0.8\%, +2.5\% and -0.0\% on REFIT, SustDataED2 and UK-DALE, and the F1 stays within 0.01 of the FP32 value. The choice of the calibration matters: KL clipping is equally good on REFIT (+0.7\%) but raises the SustDataED2 MAE by 33\%, which ACIQ clipping avoids (+2.5\%). Keeping the input projection (and, where the validation split favors it, the output convolutions) in FP32 --- a choice made on the validation split only --- keeps every regression model at or below +0.4\% of the FP32 MAE (REFIT: 2.0\% below) while the weights stay below 0.9~MB. State detection with the classification networks is more sensitive to quantization: with all convolutions in INT8 the macro-F1 changes by -0.128 and -0.182 (KL clipping, SustDataED2 and UK-DALE), whereas the mixed-precision models are within 0.035 of the FP32 value (0.373 versus 0.408 on SustDataED2 and 0.497 versus 0.503 on UK-DALE). On the PC one window is processed 2.4--2.7 times faster in INT8 than in FP32 (single thread); because the ARM926EJ-S has no SIMD unit, the speed-up on the F1C200s has to be measured on the board. The execution time, memory and power on the F1C200s are reported below; they were measured with the earlier deployment build of the same networks (same architecture and number of operations), and the execution time of the rebuilt INT8 files has to be re-measured because the INT8 kernels differ from the earlier ones.

\paragraph{Stage 4: Cross-Compilation for ARM Architecture}
The quantized NCNN model was cross-compiled for ARMv5TE architecture (ARM926EJ-S core) using the arm-linux-gnueabi-g++ toolchain with architecture flags \texttt{-mcpu=arm926ej-s} and \texttt{-mfloat-abi=soft} (software floating-point emulation). The F1C200s has no floating-point unit, so ``without FPU support'' means that the convolutions run in INT8 integer arithmetic while the residual floating-point operations (input normalization, quantization scales, output de-quantization and the final threshold) are executed by compiler-generated soft-float routines; their cost is negligible because they touch only the 512 inputs and 512$\times A$ outputs of a window, against $3.8\times10^{8}$ multiply--accumulate operations in the network. The NCNN inference engine was statically linked, eliminating runtime dependencies on external libraries. The resulting deployment comprised the inference executable (28 KB), model structure file (.param, 5 KB), and quantized weights (.bin, 850 KB), totaling 883 KB.

The hardware profiling results confirm successful deployment across all model variants with consistent inference characteristics. Key findings include: (1) \textbf{Model load times} of 284--400~ms demonstrate efficient model initialization. (2) \textbf{Inference timing stability}: the minimum and maximum inference times over five iterations differ by less than 0.03~s, i.e.\ the execution time is deterministic on the constrained platform and the maximum observed time (123.48~s) can be used as a worst-case execution time for scheduling in the absence of other load. CPU utilization during an inference run is 99.81\% on average (100\% peak), as expected for a single-core ARM926EJ-S without SIMD acceleration; because inference is scheduled once per reporting interval, this saturation lasts only about two minutes in every fifteen. Power consumption during active inference remained constant at 0.513~W (102.68~mA at 5~V) across all model variants, which is 85.3\% below the Raspberry Pi 4 baseline (3.48~W active) and 71.6\% below the Raspberry Pi Zero 2W (1.81~W active); these platform comparisons use the benchmarking workloads of Table~\ref{tab:hardwarebenchmarks} rather than the NILM workload, so they compare platform power rather than energy per inference.

\paragraph{Latency, duty cycle and energy}
\label{subsubsec:latency}
Because the term ``real-time'' is used for several time scales, Table~\ref{tab:latency} separates the contributions. Collecting a 512-step window takes 68.3 minutes, and after the window is complete the F1C200s needs 101--123~s to produce the 512 per-step estimates of all appliances. In steady state the NILM service therefore delivers the estimates of an interval about two minutes after the interval has closed, which is adequate for energy analytics, appliance-level feedback and billing support at the 15-minute reporting granularity. The network is almost causal: an input perturbation test on the deployed model shows that a change at step $t$ influences the outputs from step $t-4$ to step $t+256$ (261 steps, i.e.\ the 259-step backbone receptive field plus the 3-tap output convolution), so each estimate depends on at most 32~s of future samples, and a newly arriving sample can be processed incrementally by caching the activations of the preceding 256 steps. With the measured effective throughput of 3.7 million MACs per second, the incremental cost of one new 8-second step is roughly 0.74 million MACs, i.e.\ about 0.2~s, which indicates that estimates with a delay of seconds are architecturally possible; this streaming mode has not been implemented or measured, and the batch mode reported here should be regarded as the deployed configuration. Sub-minute event services (for example demand-response triggers) are therefore outside what the present batch implementation claims.

The complete pipeline, from PyTorch training to ARM binary deployment, required no model architecture modifications, confirming the portability of the EDTCN design across compute platforms.

\paragraph{Accuracy of the quantized models}
The accuracy of the FP32 and INT8 networks is evaluated on the complete test splits (Table~\ref{tab:int8_pc}) with the same NCNN network files that are deployed; this evaluation, and not a subset, is the basis of the accuracy statements of this paper. The FP32 NCNN models reproduce the metrics of the trained PyTorch checkpoints exactly (REFIT 15.66~W and F1 0.448, SustDataED2 5.25~W and 0.824, UK-DALE 17.30~W and 0.531), so the conversion to NCNN is lossless, and the INT8 changes are those quantified in Section~\ref{subsubsec:deployment_details}: regression models stay within 0.4\% (mixed precision) or within 2.5\% (all layers in INT8 with ACIQ clipping) of the FP32 MAE, and the classification models need the input and output convolutions in FP32 to stay close to the FP32 macro-F1.

\paragraph{Hardware Deployment Summary}
The deployment validation supports four conclusions. (1) \textbf{Accuracy}: quantization to INT8 changes the regression MAE by at most a few percent and, with mixed precision, by less than 0.4\% on all three datasets, evaluated on the complete test splits. (2) \textbf{Size}: the weights shrink 3.9 times to 0.72--0.98~MB. (3) \textbf{Execution on the F1C200s}: one 512-step window is processed in 101--123~s with 66.77\% memory utilization and 0.513~W board-level power, i.e.\ 33--40 times faster than the data rate and an 11--14\% CPU duty cycle at a 15-minute cadence; these on-device values were measured with the earlier deployment build of the networks. (4) \textbf{Pipeline}: the chain PyTorch training $\rightarrow$ PNNX $\rightarrow$ NCNN INT8 $\rightarrow$ ARM binary required no change of the network architecture, apart from expressing the 1-D convolutions as equivalent 2-D convolutions so that the NCNN tools can quantize them.

\subsubsection{Summary and Key Findings}
\label{subsubsec:nilm_summary}

\textit{Accuracy.} Under a documented chronological and household-level protocol, the EDTCN reaches $5.34\pm0.10$~W MAE (F1 0.814) on SustDataED2, $15.38\pm0.21$~W (F1 0.464) on REFIT and $15.01\pm0.15$~W (F1 0.549) on UK-DALE (mean $\pm$ std over seeds), which is 9--26\% below the strongest of three baselines re-implemented and trained under the identical protocol ($p<0.001$ in every comparison). The ablation attributes the gain mainly to exponentially dilated residual blocks with a 259-step (34.5-minute) receptive field; the device-type-weighted focal loss does not lower the MAE but raises the recall of regular-use and burst appliances, whereas MAE-only training gives the lowest MAE at the price of underestimated energy.

\textit{Evaluation.} Reporting MAE together with RMSE, SAE, NDE, precision, recall and F1 for every appliance exposes behavior that MAE hides: the 30.6\% pooled precision of the unthresholded REFIT regression output, the near-zero F1 of very low-power and rarely active appliances, and the difference between the pooled and the macro-averaged F1. A post-processing threshold selected on the validation split trades recall for precision at no cost on the meter, and the F1 is stable over a broad range of thresholds.

\textit{Robustness.} A REFIT house that was not seen in training costs on average about 1~W of MAE (leave-one-house-out); zero-shot transfer between REFIT and UK-DALE retains 86--88\% of the in-domain F1 on the shared appliances; and adaptation with one to seven days of target data lowers the MAE, with elastic weight consolidation limiting the forgetting of the source domain for short adaptation periods. Very low-power devices ($<$2~W) and extremely rare appliances (ON time $<$0.5\%) remain hard to detect and call for event-based detection strategies, which are future work. The embedded deployment of the trained models is described in the following section.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpreting the NILM Results}
\label{subsec:interpretation}

\textit{Temporal context drives accuracy.} Appliance activity unfolds over very different time scales, from the minutes of a kettle cycle to the tens of minutes of a washing-machine program. With the depth and the loss held fixed, enlarging the receptive field through exponential dilation from 31 steps (plain and residual TCN) to 259 steps lowers the MAE by 11--12\% on both datasets, and extending it from 67 to 259 steps helps on UK-DALE (1.2~W), whose washing machine and dishwasher run long programs, but not on SustDataED2, whose appliances are dominated by short bursts and steady loads. The Seq2Point-style baseline has a receptive field of only 30 steps (4 minutes), which is consistent with its higher error.

\textit{Residual connections need dilation.} Residual connections alone leave the receptive field at 31 steps and brought no improvement in the ablation (and made training on SustDataED2 unstable), whereas with dilation they allow six blocks to be trained reliably, as the small spread across seeds (0.1--0.2~W) shows.

\textit{The loss selects an operating point.} The minimizer of an absolute-error loss is the conditional median, which for an appliance that is OFF most of the time lies close to zero; MAE training therefore reaches the lowest MAE but underestimates the energy (SAE 0.46--0.54 versus 0.19--0.25 for the other losses). Squared-error terms pull the estimate toward the conditional mean and preserve energy, and the device-type weights and the focal factor additionally emphasize large relative errors on regular-use and burst appliances, which raises their recall (REFIT regular-use appliances from 0.52 to 0.71) at a MAE cost of 0.4--0.7~W. Which loss is preferable depends on the service: energy accuracy for billing and feedback, recall for event-driven functions such as demand response.

\textit{Datasets differ in the signal, not only in the model.} The SustDataED2 aggregate is the sum of the metered appliances and contains no unmetered load, and its appliances have distinct power levels; REFIT and UK-DALE contain unmetered consumption and overlapping signatures (dishwasher, washing machine and tumble dryer all draw 500--2500~W). Errors should therefore be compared within a dataset, and the state-detection results transfer better across datasets than the amplitude: the physical signature of a compressor cycle or a kettle step is similar in every household, whereas the MAE also depends on the specific appliance models and on the background load, which a few days of adaptation can correct.


\subsection{Operational Scalability and Edge Efficiency}
\label{subsec:scalability}

The transition from the Second Generation prototype to the Third Generation industrial design represents a critical step toward the mass scalability of the NGSM system. The benchmark results presented in Section \ref{sec:hardware} demonstrate that the custom-fabricated node achieves a 35.9\% reduction in idle current (86.00 versus 134.24 mA) and a 71.6\% reduction in active current (102.68 versus 361.48 mA) compared to the discrete single-board computer architecture. This efficiency is paramount for utility providers, as the aggregate power consumption of widespread smart meter deployments constitutes a significant operational load on the grid itself.

Furthermore, the architectural shift to edge-based processing significantly alleviates the bandwidth constraints associated with traditional cloud-centric monitoring systems. Recent studies in urban computing infrastructure emphasize that decentralized processing is essential for reducing latency and improving bandwidth utilization in large-scale sensor networks \cite{Kelly2024}. By executing the high-frequency NILM inference locally and transmitting only the classification results (approximately 1 KB per event) rather than the raw 16 kHz waveforms, the system reduces data transmission requirements by several orders of magnitude. Additionally, the migration to onboard SPI NAND Flash and the SiP design enhances the device's reliability in harsh industrial environments.

\subsection{Adaptation and the Cold-Start Problem}
\label{subsec:privacy_nilm}

A persistent barrier to the adoption of NILM is the cold-start problem, where a model trained on other households must be used in a new one. The experiments of Section~\ref{subsubsec:robustness} quantify it for the proposed system: without any target data the source models retain 86--88\% of the in-domain F1 on the shared appliances, a REFIT house held out of training costs on average about 1~W of MAE, and adaptation with one to seven days of target data lowers the MAE (by up to 6~W on REFIT house 21) while elastic weight consolidation limits, but does not eliminate, the forgetting of the source domain. Adaptation does not improve every appliance: the UK-DALE dishwasher, which runs for about 2\% of the time, is not improved by a week of data. Adaptation is computed off the meter and only the exported INT8 model is installed, so the constrained hardware is not burdened; how updated models are distributed to many meters is a deployment question that is outside the scope of this study.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

The NILM evaluation has the following limits. The SustDataED2 aggregate is the sum of the metered appliance channels, a closed setting without unmetered load; SustDataED2 and UK-DALE are single households, so unseen-household behavior is measured only on REFIT; the REFIT models of the controlled experiments use a reduced training protocol; the baselines are our own re-implementations and were not individually tuned; the cross-dataset and adaptation study covers five shared appliances and two source--target pairs; and neither multi-year drift nor changes in the appliance inventory could be assessed with the available data. Single-phase residential loads are considered; three-phase and industrial installations, other grid standards and larger appliance sets remain to be studied.

Despite the achievements presented in this work, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the edge deployment on the F1C200s saturates the single core (99.81\% average, 100\% peak) while an inference runs. Because inference is executed once per 15-minute reporting interval and takes 101--123~s (an 11--14\% duty cycle), the saturation is confined to about two minutes in every fifteen and the remaining time is available to the other tasks; running the metrology, display and communication tasks concurrently with an inference at low priority is the design intent, and its measurement under load is part of the planned field validation. The batch implementation delivers results about two minutes after each interval and does not target sub-minute event detection; an incremental, activation-caching implementation of the almost causal network is a natural next step toward that goal. Another limitation is that the energy demand forecasting module currently relies on cloud offloading, introducing a dependency on network connectivity and external infrastructure. While this architectural decision was made to preserve edge computational resources, it represents a potential single point of failure for predictive analytics in scenarios with intermittent connectivity.

Future work should target several interrelated improvements. First, mitigating CPU saturation through advanced model optimization including hardware-aware neural architecture search, structured pruning, and knowledge distillation to smaller, efficient models promises to preserve disaggregation accuracy while reducing inference time on constrained devices. Second, expanding the federated learning framework to hierarchical, multi-meter coordination could enable aggregate consumption analytics at transformer or feeder levels, improving utility-side use cases such as demand response and loss detection without exposing raw household data; hierarchical FL has been shown to address data privacy, latency, and communication constraints in IoT contexts. Third, integrating lightweight, on-device forecasting models based on recent efficient time-series architectures would shift predictive capability from the cloud to the meter itself, supporting autonomous edge prediction under network constraints. Fourth, the security posture of the embedded system requires formal evaluation--despite inherent privacy advantages of local data retention, embedded Linux environments and update mechanisms introduce distributed attack surfaces that must be hardened through systematic penetration testing. Finally, pilot field deployments with utility partners are needed to gather empirical data on long-term reliability, model drift in real operating conditions, and user acceptance of appliance-level energy visibility.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work presented NGSM, a holistic hardware–software framework that bridges the gap between low-power embedded metering and high-performance energy analytics. By moving from general-purpose prototyping platforms to a custom Allwinner F1C200s System-in-Package, the system achieves substantial efficiency gains, reducing active power consumption to 0.513W (102.68 mA at 5V), which is 85.3\% below a Raspberry Pi 4 and 71.6\% below a Raspberry Pi Zero 2W baseline. At the algorithmic level, an Enhanced Dilated Temporal Convolutional Network (EDTCN) with 707K parameters was designed for embedded NILM inference and validated across SustDataED2, REFIT, and UK-DALE datasets. Under a documented protocol the EDTCN reaches $5.34\pm0.10$~W, $15.38\pm0.21$~W and $15.01\pm0.15$~W MAE with F1-scores of 0.81, 0.46 and 0.55 (mean $\pm$ std over seeds), 9--26\% below the strongest of three baselines trained under the identical protocol. The ablation attributes the gain to the dilated residual architecture, while the device-type-weighted focal loss raises the recall of regular-use and burst appliances. A held-out household costs about 1~W of MAE, zero-shot transfer retains 86--88\% of the in-domain F1, and a few days of adaptation with elastic weight consolidation reduce the error with limited forgetting. Calibrated INT8 quantization reduces the weight storage by a factor of 3.9 to below 1~MB with regression MAE within 3\% of FP32 on the complete test splits, which enables deployment on 64MB RAM without GPU or FPU support. A key finding of this work is that commonly reported metrics such as MAE alone can be misleading in NILM evaluation, as low aggregate error may coincide with poor event-level detection performance. Accordingly, this study adopts a comprehensive multi-metric evaluation framework, including MAE, RMSE, SAE, NDE, precision, recall, and F1-score, across all appliances and datasets, providing a more transparent assessment of model behavior, including failure cases. Beyond disaggregation, NGSM integrates on-device FFT-based harmonic analysis for IEEE 519-compliant power quality monitoring and a cloud-assisted forecasting module, illustrating a practical edge–cloud partitioning strategy for resource-constrained environments. Overall, NGSM demonstrates that appliance-level energy analytics with near-real-time delivery can be achieved under strict embedded constraints, offering a scalable and reproducible pathway toward decentralized smart grid intelligence.
